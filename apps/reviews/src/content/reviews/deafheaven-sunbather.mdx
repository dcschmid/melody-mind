## Longing enters through the loudest door

_Sunbather_ begins at a level many albums reserve for their ending. “Dream House” places
tremolo-picked guitars, blast beats, and George Clarke's high rasp in immediate
competition for space. There is no gradual invitation into Deafheaven's second album. The
first task is to distinguish movement inside an already saturated field.

That density can suggest a simple genre equation: black metal force plus shoegaze color.
The record is more useful when heard as a study of distance. Clarke's lyrics circle
wealth, romantic attachment, family, and versions of a stable life that remain visible but
unreachable. Kerry McCoy's guitars often make that distance sound radiant. Open voicings
and high melodic lines promise release while distortion keeps them physically out of
reach.

The four long songs do not solve the conflict. They change how close the listener seems to
stand. Three shorter pieces interrupt their scale, clear the rhythmic field, and make the
next impact feel earned. Intensity becomes a location, not a verdict. The album keeps
leaving it and returning with a different reason.

## Bright harmony survives the blast

Deafheaven recorded the album with George Clarke, Kerry McCoy, and drummer Daniel Tracy.
McCoy layered rhythm and lead parts, sometimes using a whammy bar to pull whole chords
slightly out of tune. Jack Shirley recorded the sessions to two-inch tape before editing
and mixing in Pro Tools. That combination matters less as an analog-versus-digital story
than as a way of keeping abundance organized.

The guitars can shimmer, bend, or gather into a broad major-colored field without losing
their abrasive edge. Tracy gives those layers direction. His cymbal patterns mark the top
of the sound, while the kick and snare identify whether a passage is driving, hovering, or
preparing to break open. Brightness does not soften the attack. It gives the attack
something emotionally unstable to carry.

### Dream House: Release arrives before relief

“Dream House” moves through several recognizable phases without treating them as separate
songs. Its opening rush has a strong forward pull, but the guitar figures rise across the
beat instead of locking into a blunt riff. Clarke's voice is another line of pressure, not
a narrator positioned clearly in front of the band.

When the blast recedes, the guitars widen and the pulse becomes easier to feel. The change
resembles release, yet it does not create calm. The closing vocal lines came from a
private exchange that Clarke connected to love and death. Their return over the broad
final progression makes intimacy sound overwhelming rather than secure. The section is
memorable because the music opens before the feeling does.

### Sunbather: Envy stretched across open chords

Clarke described the title image as an ideal of beauty and wealth that he could see but
not inhabit. The title track turns that social distance into duration. Its guitar pattern
is luminous enough to suggest arrival, while the screamed vocal and relentless drumming
deny any stable possession of the scene.

The long form allows attraction to become irritation and then exhaustion. Deafheaven does
not introduce a darker riff to expose brightness as false. The same open harmonic color
persists under increasing pressure. Envy is heard as the inability to leave a beautiful
image alone. By the time the track loosens its grip, the image has become a measure of
everything the narrator lacks.

## Four long songs need three smaller rooms

“Irresistible,” “Please Remember,” and “Windows” are not equal miniature songs, nor are
they neutral pauses. They alter the implied size of the album. Clean guitar, keyboard,
noise, and recorded voices bring the listener closer to individual surfaces after the long
tracks have made every gesture feel panoramic.

The sequence depends on those reductions. Without “Irresistible,” the entrance of the
title track would be another immediate appeal to maximum scale. Without “Windows,” “The
Pecan Tree” would follow the album's longest piece before the ear had recovered enough to
register a new attack. The interludes create thresholds rather than relief.

### Please Remember: A voice interrupts the scale

“Please Remember” makes that function explicit. A spoken passage by Stéphane Paut enters
with none of Clarke's physical strain. The difference in delivery briefly restores the
semantic surface that the screamed vocals often surrender. The voice sounds placed in a
room rather than projected across a horizon.

Noise eventually crowds the speech, and the track moves toward a solitary guitar figure.
The parts do not resolve into a compact composition. They interrupt one another, leaving a
residue of unease before “Vertigo.” That rough handoff changes the listener's footing; a
smooth ambient bridge would merely promise that the next large build will be safe.

## Daniel Tracy keeps the horizon moving

The album's guitar color attracted much of the discussion around _Sunbather_, but its
hour-long shape depends on Tracy. Blast beats can erase hierarchy when every accent
arrives as part of the same rush. Tracy repeatedly prevents that. Ride and crash patterns
outline phrases, fills announce structural hinges, and changes in kick-drum density tell
the listener whether a guitar layer is pushing forward or spreading outward.

That control matters most in the long middle passages. McCoy can sustain a harmonic field
while the drums change its purpose underneath. A figure first heard as atmosphere can
become a lead when the snare pattern clears space around it. A broad climax can fall back
into motion without requiring a completely new riff.

Clarke's vocal offers less contrast. His high, torn register is essential to the album's
friction, but it gives confession, accusation, and memory a similar surface. The words
reward reading, particularly when they make class desire or family damage specific. Inside
the mix, however, shifts in lyrical perspective are harder to hear than shifts in drumming
or guitar register. The arrangement often has to communicate changes that the voice
cannot.

## One climax begins to resemble another

Deafheaven's large-scale method is persuasive enough to become predictable. A clean or
reduced passage establishes room; guitars gather in rising layers; drums increase the rate
of impact; a bright progression arrives at extreme volume. The emotional meaning can
change, but the physical route sometimes does not.

### Vertigo: Expansion reaches its limit

At more than fourteen minutes, “Vertigo” has the space to expose that limit. Its patient
opening and gradual additions create real suspense. Tracy keeps revising the pulse, and
McCoy's layered parts move between wavering texture and defined melodic lines. When the
full band arrives, the earlier restraint gives the impact a clear memory.

The later stages keep enlarging gestures that are already complete. Another rise in guitar
register and another surge of percussion confirm magnitude rather than changing the
track's emotional position. The performance remains forceful, but scale starts to
substitute for a new decision. Because the album has taught the listener how carefully a
change in pressure can redirect meaning, repetition here is easier to notice.

## The Pecan Tree closes without consolation

“The Pecan Tree” answers “Vertigo” by making its contrasts more severe. The opening attack
is compact and hostile. A quieter middle section then lowers the voice and lets the
guitars hold notes long enough for their edges to become audible. The final expansion
draws those states together without pretending that brightness has repaired the damage.

Refusing consolation gives the ending its force. The wide guitar voicings can sound
victorious, but Clarke's delivery remains raw and the drums keep pressing rather than
settling into a ceremonial half-time close. Desire has not reached its object. It has
acquired a form large enough to be shared.

_Sunbather_ lasts because its familiar opposition—beauty against abrasion—is only the
surface of its construction. The record's deeper movement comes from changes of distance.
A long song turns an ideal over until attraction becomes pain. An interlude makes the room
small enough to hear a single voice. A drummer changes what a sustained guitar field is
doing without clearing it away. The album occasionally repeats its route to grandeur, and
its vocal extremity narrows the words it wants to expose. Even so, its best passages make
maximum volume remarkably precise: not a wall around longing, but the measure of how far
longing has to travel.

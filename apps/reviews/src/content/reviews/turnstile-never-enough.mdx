## Openness as a rhythmic decision

Turnstile's problem after _Glow On_ was formal rather than commercial. The band had
already reached audiences that hardcore rarely finds without changing its basic social
charge. The question was how much additional space the songs could hold before impact
became atmosphere.

_Never Enough_ answers unevenly. Its guitars are wider, its edges are smoother, and
textures often remain after a riff has stopped. The playing still depends on compression
and release. Daniel Fang's drums locate the body, Franz Lyons gives the guitar a moving
floor, and Brendan Yates keeps most melodies short enough to retain the directness of a
crowd response.

Openness changes rhythm when a frequency is cleared, an entrance is delayed, or a low
instrument receives enough room to redirect the groove. A synth wash or clean guitar can
also leave the arrangement drifting when it mainly advertises the band's range.

## A hardcore record with more rooms

The expansion did not begin here. _Glow On_ had already placed percussion breaks, large
melodic choruses, and dream-pop color beside the band's hardcore attack. On _Never
Enough_, those openings are no longer exceptions. They are the normal conditions under
which Turnstile writes, so each added texture has to do more than surprise by appearing.

Yates produced the album, with Will Yip credited for additional production and
engineering. The arrangements also use contributors for specific colors: Devonté Hynes
plays cello on the title track and “Look Out for Me”; Leland Whitty and Kae Murphy add
horns to “Dreaming”; Shabaka Hutchings plays flute on “Sunshower.” The credits read like
an expanded palette, but the useful distinction is audible function. A guest part matters
when it changes the scale or direction of a song.

The distinction exposes both the album's confidence and its unevenness. Turnstile can let
a guitar ring or a vocal soften without losing contact because bass and drums keep
defining forward motion. When the arrangement widens but the next riff returns to familiar
work, the color remains external to the composition.

## Four ways the frame expands

### Never Enough: The kick anchors the wider frame

The title track spreads guitar across the stereo field and gives Yates more room for a
sustained melody. The production is broad, but Fang's kick remains dry and decisive. Each
low hit redraws the distance between voice and guitar, preventing the chorus from floating
into generic scale.

The credits identify Yates as producer, with Will Yip providing additional production and
engineering. That division fits what the song sounds like: the arrangement reaches outward
while the band's internal rhythmic language remains intact. Added cello from Devonté Hynes
widens the color without replacing the pulse.

### Sole: Bass carries the chorus

“Sole” is a better test of the album's polish than its surface suggests. Lyons does not
settle for root notes under the bright guitar layers. His bass line pushes through the
chorus, adding forward pull where a simpler part would let the arrangement turn
weightless.

The song shows how Turnstile's hardcore background persists as mechanics rather than a
genre badge. Guitar can ring and Yates can soften his delivery because bass and drums keep
the point of contact exact. The wider mix changes the space around the body; it does not
remove the body.

### Look Out for Me: Atmosphere changes the form

The first half moves with the compact force expected of the band. The extended second half
changes the track's time scale: rhythm becomes less directive, cello and electronics widen
the field, and the warning in Yates's vocal hangs after the earlier urgency has stopped
driving it.

The ambient outro changes how the first section is remembered, holding its alarm in
suspension. Added space produces a formal consequence instead of merely advertising range.

### Slowdive: Soft focus repeats an established idea

“Slowdive” uses clean guitar, softened vocals, and controlled distance with considerable
finish. By this point, however, the album has already made space alter form on “Look Out
for Me” and has used guest color more specifically elsewhere. The quieter surface no
longer produces the same friction.

The predictable contrast limits the track. Atmosphere remains around the song instead of
changing its mechanics. Across the album, freedom of palette matters only when a new color
changes time, pressure, or memory.

## What survives the wider palette

The rhythm section preserves Turnstile's direct physical logic inside wider arrangements.
Fang's kick can anchor a panoramic chorus, while Lyons's moving bass prevents bright
guitar layers from losing forward pull. The band does not need to imitate an earlier
hardcore record to retain that contact.

The added colors land when they have defined jobs. Hynes's cello extends the low space
around “Never Enough” and “Look Out for Me”; the latter uses that space to change its time
scale. A cleared frequency or delayed entrance can change how the next impact lands.

## When possibility becomes clutter

Several textures announce possibility without changing the song beneath them. “Slowdive”
uses soft focus attractively, but its contrast is already familiar by the time it arrives.
“Ceiling” suspends the album for barely more than a minute, but “Seein' Stars” resumes
with a self-contained pulse and arrangement. The pause separates the tracks without
altering the second one's mechanics.

The smoother palette also reduces friction between styles. On _Glow On_, an open melodic
passage could feel like a breach in the band's attack. Here openness is expected, so it
must carry more compositional weight. The album does not always meet that higher burden.

## The door stays open

_Never Enough_ treats expansion as a practical problem rather than proof of maturity. Its
sharpest songs give space a rhythmic function: the body remains located even when the
arrangement spreads outward. Elsewhere, available color is mistaken for changed form.
Turnstile's new range is real when each addition changes pressure, time, or memory.

Turnstile's problem after _Glow On_ was not how to become larger. The band had already
crossed into rooms and audiences that hardcore rarely reaches without changing its basic
social charge. The harder problem was formal: how much new space could the music hold
before impact became atmosphere?

_Never Enough_ answers unevenly but often persuasively. The record is wider, smoother at
its edges, and more willing to let a texture remain after the riff has stopped. Yet the
playing still depends on compression and release. Drums locate the body, bass gives the
guitar a moving floor, and Brendan Yates keeps melodies short enough to survive a crowd
without turning every chorus into a slogan.

The context matters because the expansion did not begin here. _Glow On_ had already placed
dream-pop color, percussion breaks, and large melodic choruses beside the band's hardcore
attack. _Never Enough_ is not another sudden crossover. It is the follow-up question: what
happens when those openings stop feeling exceptional and become the normal room in which
Turnstile writes? The answer changes the burden on each texture. A synth wash or clean
guitar no longer surprises by appearing. It has to shape time, redirect a transition, or
alter the weight of the next hit. The album succeeds whenever its added space performs
that work. It drifts when space is treated as proof of range.

<h2 id="cue-one">Listening cue 1: The kick inside the title track</h2>

The opening title track grows through addition. A repeating vocal phrase and broad guitar
field suggest an anthem, but the kick drum prevents the arrangement from floating away.
Listen to how each low hit redraws the distance between voice and guitar. The production
may be panoramic; the rhythmic instruction remains immediate.

That relationship is more important than the song's size. Many rock records use expanded
production to certify ambition. Turnstile uses it to test whether the same physical pulse
can hold a longer horizon. Here it can. The chorus lands because the drum pattern has kept
a route open through the texture.

<h2 id="cue-two">Listening cue 2: The bass through “Sole”</h2>

“Sole” is a useful check on the album's new polish. Follow the bass rather than the vocal.
It moves independently through the chorus, thickening the guitar without letting the
brighter surface become weightless. The song's melody is generous, but its momentum comes
from a low part that refuses to sit still.

This is where the band's hardcore background remains audible without becoming a genre
badge. A short song can ease its attack when the point of contact remains exact. Guitar
can ring, vocals can soften, and a small electronic detail can cross the frame because
bass and drums keep defining what counts as forward.

The album is less convincing when those additions arrive without a comparable change in
the song's mechanics. A texture opens, a transition widens, and the following riff returns
to familiar work. Range is audible, but consequence is not always.

<h2 id="cue-three">Listening cue 3: The turn in “Look Out for Me”</h2>

“Look Out for Me” earns its atmosphere because the later passage changes the time scale of
the track. The shift is not a decorative outro pasted onto a hardcore song. It asks the
listener to hold the earlier urgency in suspension. Rhythm becomes less directive, space
around the vocal grows, and the song's warning acquires a second meaning.

That is the productive version of Turnstile's expansion. Contrast does not prove that the
band can play another style. It changes how the first part is remembered. The record's
weaker bridges and ambient edges sometimes stop at color; this one alters form.

<h2 id="complete-sequence">What every track contributes</h2>

The title track and “Sole” establish the album's basic bargain: the guitars can widen and
the vocal can carry more melody, provided Daniel Fang and Franz Lyons keep the music
moving from below. “I Care” tests that bargain with bright, clipped guitar and a chorus
that leans closer to alternative rock than hardcore. The contrast is useful, although the
song's polished surface reveals less on repeat listens than its rhythm guitar does.

“Dreaming” makes a better case for the expanded palette. Its buoyant rhythm and keyboard
color do not arrive as an interlude from the band's physical language; they redirect it.
“Light Design” then reduces the pressure and lets texture lead. It is carefully placed,
but it functions more as a corridor than a destination. The album needs the change in
light even if the song does not develop far beyond it.

“Dull” restores blunt impact. Its heavier accents work because the preceding tracks have
cleared space around them, not because the band has suddenly returned to an earlier
version of itself. “Sunshower” is quicker and more compressed. Fang's drumming keeps its
bright guitar from turning weightless, though the hook resolves exactly where the
arrangement suggests it will.

“Look Out for Me” is the hinge. Its long second half changes the scale of the record and
makes the following “Ceiling” feel less like another ambient detour. “Ceiling” holds voice
and guitar at a distance, suspending the forward drive rather than cancelling it. The pair
is stronger together than either would be in isolation.

“Seein' Stars” and “Birds” form the clearest internal argument. The first uses clean,
springy guitar and a spacious chorus; the second answers with a short, hard collision.
Putting them together prevents openness and aggression from becoming separate lanes.
“Slowdive” is less decisive. Its soft focus is attractive, but it repeats an atmospheric
possibility the album has already demonstrated with more structural purpose.

“Time Is Happening” brings pulse back to the foreground without forcing a conventional
finale. “Magic Man” closes with Brendan Yates and synthesizer rather than a full-band
release. The choice is coherent: an album concerned with how far Turnstile can open its
frame ends after the frame has almost disappeared. It is also a limit. The closer leaves
an afterimage, not a final rhythmic argument.

_Never Enough_ tests which parts of Turnstile's physical language remain useful in a wider
frame. The answer is clearest in the rhythm section. When drums and bass keep the body in
view, the open space feels earned. When they recede only to display possibility, the
record briefly sounds impressed by its own freedom.

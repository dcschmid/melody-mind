## Disorder under command

“Land of Sunshine” begins with Billy Gould's bass and Mike Bordin's drums behaving as if
the song will remain a hard funk exercise. Jim Martin's guitar adds blunt weight. Then
Mike Patton enters with the smile of a motivational speaker whose advice has become a
threat. His diction is clean, his pitch exaggerated, and his breaths sound placed rather
than spontaneous.

Patton's performance helps _Angel Dust_ hold together through its abrupt turns. The album
moves through metal, lounge music, hardcore, theatrical pop, and soundtrack instrumentals,
but it is rarely uncontrolled. Gould and Bordin set exact physical terms; Roddy Bottum's
keyboards alter the room; Martin's guitar supplies friction; Patton assigns each section a
character who wants something from the listener.

The record's grotesque quality comes from discipline. Familiar voices of confidence,
desire, and reassurance are pushed until their need for control becomes audible.

## The difficult follow-up

_Angel Dust_ followed the commercial reach of _The Real Thing_ and was the first Faith No
More album on which Patton participated substantially in the writing. The original
thirteen-track edition was released by Slash and Reprise on June 8, 1992. The band and
Matt Wallace produced it, recording at Coast Recorders and Brilliant Studios in San
Francisco.

The lineup's internal differences became part of the music. Gould, Bordin, Bottum, and
Patton pursued arrangements that could change style without warning, while Martin's guitar
often sounds like a resistant block inside them. That friction complicates a clean story
of reinvention. The metal riffing becomes one voice among keyboards, samples, bass-led
grooves, and tightly arranged vocal scenes.

Current digital editions commonly append the Commodores cover “Easy.” This review treats
“Midnight Cowboy” as the close of the original thirteen-track CD sequence. The distinction
matters because the instrumental ends the album by withdrawing language, not by adding a
knowing encore.

## Four scenes of managed instability

### Caffeine: Repetition tightens the room

“Caffeine” builds pressure from a bass figure and drum pattern that refuse to settle into
an easy backbeat. Martin's guitar attacks in short blocks, while Bottum's keyboard adds an
industrial glare around the riff. Patton moves from clipped speech to a strained upper
register without presenting either as the natural voice of the song.

The arrangement does not chase complexity through constant new material. It repeats until
the room feels smaller. Changes in vocal register and guitar density make the same pulse
more oppressive. This is the album's control principle in its least comic form.

### Midlife Crisis: A loop becomes a trap

The opening drum loop gives “Midlife Crisis” a mechanical steadiness, but Gould's bass
keeps nudging against it. Patton delivers the verse in a low, measured register, then
opens the chorus into a broader melody that sounds triumphant only from a distance.
Bottum's keyboard line brightens the surface without resolving the tension underneath.

The song is accessible because every layer has a legible job. It is unsettling because
those jobs do not agree. Rhythm promises continuity, the chorus promises release, and the
voice treats both promises with suspicion.

### RV: The powerless man controls the monologue

“RV” removes the band's usual attack. Piano, restrained bass, and a dragging beat support
Patton's nasal portrait of a man sunk into his chair and his resentments. The vocal is a
caricature, but the arrangement gives it patience. No loud chorus arrives to tell the
listener how to judge him.

That refusal makes the track more than comic relief. The character has little social
power, yet he controls the room by refusing movement. When the band finally increases the
pressure, the shift exposes the aggression already present in the quieter delivery.

### A Small Victory: Precision makes optimism suspicious

Handclap-like percussion, bright keyboards, and a sharply articulated bass line give “A
Small Victory” forward motion. Patton's melody is one of the album's most open, but the
phrasing remains stiff enough to resist uncomplicated uplift. Each syllable sounds
inspected before it leaves his mouth.

The track shows how the band can use polish without becoming reassuring. Its parts are
cleaner than those of “Caffeine,” yet their exact alignment feels ceremonial. The victory
in the title is bounded and provisional; the arrangement keeps measuring it.

## A rhythm section that permits excess

Gould and Bordin make the album's range credible. The bass often carries the identifiable
shape of a song while guitar and keyboards change its surface. Bordin can make a loop feel
physical, hold a slow character piece together, or drive a heavy section without
flattening its syncopation. Their precision lets the record cut between styles without
losing its body.

Patton's vocal control supplies the other continuity. He does not simply demonstrate a
large range. He chooses social voices: salesman, scold, crooner, patient, braggart. The
changes reveal how performance can turn authority into panic. Bottum's keyboards sharpen
that effect by invoking familiar spaces, from arena sheen to television comfort, then
making them slightly hostile.

## Caricature can close the question

The same theatrical method sometimes reaches its conclusion too quickly. “Be Aggressive”
is arranged with memorable choral force, but its provocation can dominate the musical
development. Once the joke and its target are clear, repetition adds scale more readily
than ambiguity.

“Malpractice” pushes fragmentation further, placing violent ensemble blocks beside sampled
and orchestral color. Its instability suits the album, though some transitions register as
interruption before they become composition. The record's control is never in doubt; the
reason for a particular collision is occasionally thinner.

The cover of John Barry's “Midnight Cowboy” provides an elegant exit, but elegance is also
its limitation. After an album that makes reassurance suspect, the instrumental closes the
door with unusual neatness.

## The mechanism remains exposed

_Angel Dust_ holds together because the band gives every rupture a frame. Bass and drums
define the floor, keyboards change the setting, guitar pushes against the arrangement, and
Patton decides which unreliable person is speaking.

Some characters become cartoons, and a few collisions advertise difference more clearly
than they develop it. The stronger songs keep their mechanism exposed. They allow the
listener to hear discipline producing disgust, humor, and unease at the same time. What
first resembles chaos is a band tightening its grip.

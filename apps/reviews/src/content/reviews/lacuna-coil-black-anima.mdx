## Grief given a heavy frame

The 11-track standard edition of _Black Anima_ begins with “Anima Nera,” a two-and-a-half-
minute prologue of distant voices, electronics, and gathering pressure. “Sword of Anger”
then replaces suggestion with a low, percussive guitar figure and Andrea Ferro's harsh
lead. Cristina Scabbia does not simply soften the impact when she enters. Her clearer
melody changes the distance from which the same darkness is heard.

Across the album, Ferro often sounds inside the immediate force of anger or panic. Scabbia
can stand within that force, answer it, or observe what remains after it passes. Lacuna
Coil has long been identified by two lead voices, but _Black Anima_ works best when the
difference affects structure rather than reproducing a familiar harsh-verse, clean-chorus
division.

The record grew out of several deaths close to the band, including the loss of both of
Scabbia's parents within months. Andrea Ferro described the concept as learning to direct
energy through events that cannot be controlled. That context matters, but the album
cannot rely on biography to make every heavy gesture meaningful. Its strongest songs turn
grief into timing, register, and vocal relation. Its weaker passages settle for weight as
proof of seriousness.

## The dual voice rebuilt for 2019

Marco Coti Zelati produced _Black Anima_ and recorded it at BRX Studio in Milan. Marco
Barusso mixed the album there, and Marco D'Agostino mastered it at 96kHz Mastering Studio.
The production places guitars and bass in a dense low block while preserving a separate
space for electronics, choral textures, and the two singers. The sound is polished, but
its edges remain deliberate.

The album continues the heavier direction of 2016's _Delirium_. Guitar parts favor
syncopated, palm-muted patterns and short bursts of movement over the more open gothic
rock language associated with parts of Lacuna Coil's earlier catalog. The rhythm section
often treats the riff as percussion. Keyboards do important atmospheric work around that
impact, especially in introductions and transitions.

Ferro's vocal approach has also changed from the restrained male countervoice heard on the
band's early records. His growls now carry entire sections with a coarse, compressed
force. Scabbia answers with sustained melodic lines whose clarity is heightened by the low
instrumental register. The opposition is effective because neither voice belongs
exclusively to aggression or beauty. On the better tracks, each singer can sound wounded,
commanding, or detached.

The prologue and closing title track frame the album as a passage through a dark soul.
Ferro's account of the record as a book of presences gives that shape a documented
conceptual basis. Within the sequence, however, the individual chapters are linked more by
atmosphere and recurring vocal roles than by a legible narrative.

## Four points where darkness takes shape

### Sword of Anger: The body arrives before the explanation

After the suspended prologue, “Sword of Anger” lands on a riff whose low notes function
almost like additional kick drums. Ferro enters close to the instrumental attack. His
phrases are short and forceful, keeping the verse physically compressed.

Scabbia's chorus widens the melodic range without removing the pressure underneath. The
contrast makes anger sound less stable, as if the song can feel the cost of its own
forward motion. Repeated exchanges retain their effect because the arrangement changes
space around the voices instead of simply alternating them.

### Layers of Time: Two singers share one acceleration

“Layers of Time” starts quickly and keeps its guitar figure in tight rhythmic relation
with the drums. Ferro's opening is urgent, while Scabbia's refrain lengthens the vocal
line across the same pulse. The chorus feels larger because of duration and register, not
because the band abandons the riff.

Electronic color sits behind the guitars, the rhythm remains sharply defined, and the
vocal handoff turns a compact arrangement into a change of perspective. Once the chorus
has established the pattern, however, later returns confirm rather than deepen it.

### Under the Surface: Melody carries the concealed pressure

“Under the Surface” gives Scabbia more sustained control. The verse leaves pockets around
her lead, and the chorus uses a direct melodic rise instead of competing with the low
guitar mass. Ferro's presence feels like pressure entering from the edge rather than a
required counterweight.

The track reduces blunt impact without becoming a separate ballad, giving the sequence a
different pace. It also exposes how conventional some melodic resolutions can be. Scabbia
gives the chorus emotional precision, but the chord movement reaches a familiar uplift
that the surrounding unease has not fully earned.

### Veneficium: Ritual atmosphere changes the scale

At more than six minutes, “Veneficium” has room to let sound gather. Choral and keyboard
textures create a ceremonial space before the full band establishes a slow, heavy pulse.
Scabbia's longer lines move above it while Ferro enters as a disruptive force rather than
the automatic owner of the verse.

The track's scale is justified by changes in density. Sections recede enough for the next
entrance to carry consequence, and the vocal contrast grows more complicated as the song
continues. Instead of assigning light and dark to fixed singers, the arrangement lets both
voices approach threat and mourning from different angles. The album's concept becomes
audible here without requiring an explanation.

## Contrast with real consequence

Scabbia and Ferro remain recognizable individuals throughout the production. Their voices
are not blended into a generic wall, and the songs can change direction through a vocal
entrance before the instruments move. That independence gives familiar verse-chorus forms
more dramatic information.

Coti Zelati's production also keeps the low register readable. Guitar and bass create
mass, but kick patterns, electronic pulses, and vocal consonants still mark the surface.
The record can sound dense without becoming indistinct. “Reckless” and “Apocalypse” both
benefit from that separation, particularly when melodic phrases cut through arrangements
that remain rhythmically severe.

The album's grief is strongest when restraint follows impact. Small electronic tails,
brief withdrawals of guitar, and the prologue's suspended space make heaviness measurable.
Without those contrasts, the record would confuse pressure with depth.

## Where weight becomes uniform

Several middle tracks rely on closely related low, syncopated guitar shapes. “Now or
Never,” “Under the Surface,” and “The End Is All I Can See” contain different vocal
melodies, but their physical foundations can feel interchangeable in sequence. The
production preserves detail inside each song while the album-level contour flattens.

The two-singer format sometimes hardens into expectation. A harsh entrance followed by a
clean melodic opening remains effective, yet repetition turns perspective into allocation:
Ferro supplies impact, Scabbia supplies expansion. “Veneficium” is so persuasive partly
because it complicates those jobs.

The conceptual frame has a similar gap. The prologue, title, artwork, and interviews
define a book of presences shaped by loss. Individual lyrics communicate anger, endurance,
and mortality, but the sequence does not always reveal how one chapter changes the next.
The record carries a coherent emotional climate more clearly than a developing journey.

## The soul returns altered

_Black Anima_ does not ask grief to become quiet or tasteful. It gives loss a physical
frame and trusts two very different voices to test that frame from within. The result is
most convincing when a vocal change alters the listener's position or when an electronic
withdrawal makes the next riff heavier. Repeated low patterns and predictable handoffs
occasionally narrow that range. The closing title track returns to the suspended world of
the prologue with more weight around it, leaving the album's darkness present rather than
resolved.

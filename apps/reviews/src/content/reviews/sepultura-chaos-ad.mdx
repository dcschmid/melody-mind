## The first downbeat changes the band

The opening of “Refuse/Resist” contains the argument of _Chaos A.D._ in miniature. A
heartbeat and street noise give way to Igor Cavalera's drums, then the band lands on a
riff with enough space around it for every strike to register. Earlier Sepultura records
proved how violently the group could accelerate. Here, slowing down makes the music feel
larger and more public.

That change is not retreat. Max Cavalera's short vocal lines, Andreas Kisser's sharply
bounded guitar figures, Paulo Jr.'s bass, and Igor's displaced accents turn the beat into
a point of assembly. Political resistance is carried as much by shared timing as by words.

The move is especially striking after _Beneath the Remains_ and _Arise_, albums whose
precision made acceleration a measure of authority. On _Chaos A.D._, the band no longer
needs to fill every subdivision to prove command. A rest can become a threat; a repeated
two-note figure can hold more tension than a long chromatic run. Sepultura does not become
less technical. It relocates technique from the number of events to the weight and
placement of each one.

## Weight after speed

Sepultura recorded with producer Andy Wallace after the technical extremity of _Arise_.
The twelve-track standard edition keeps thrash metal's attack but loosens its constant
forward drive. Riffs repeat long enough to become physical instructions. Cymbals and toms
occupy a wider field, while the guitar often functions as percussion with pitch rather
than an unbroken wall.

The production gives simplicity consequences. When the band changes one accent or removes
the guitar, the listener feels the floor shift. This makes _Chaos A.D._ a pivotal
groove-metal record without reducing it to a style label. Groove here is a way of making
conflict legible.

Wallace keeps the low end firm without letting it blur the pick attack. Kisser's guitar
often has a dry, serrated edge, while Igor's toms carry more resonance and apparent depth.
That separation matters on “Amen”: an opening guitar figure suspends the pulse before the
full band turns it into a heavy procession. The song can move from ominous space to massed
impact because the record has not spent all of its volume in advance.

## Rhythm as public speech

### Refuse/Resist: The snare starts a march

Igor's opening pattern refuses a square rock backbeat. Toms roll beneath the central riff,
and the snare places hard interruptions against it. Max answers in phrases short enough to
sound collective even when one person shouts them. The chorus does not explain resistance;
its paired verbs create a rhythm anyone could return. The song's economy is the source of
its scale.

### Territory: Space between the blows

“Territory” opens with a dry guitar figure and drums that imply several possible downbeats
before the full riff settles. The main pattern is simple, but its rests matter as much as
its notes. Those gaps let Igor's kick and snare argue with the guitar rather than merely
support it. Max's vocal sits above the riff like a warning issued across distance. The
arrangement turns geopolitical division into repeated musical borders: stop, strike,
withdraw, return.

### Slave New World: Compression without blur

At under three minutes, “Slave New World” restores speed without returning to the older
band's density. The verse riff stays clipped, the drums remain readable, and the chorus
widens instead of adding more notes. The track demonstrates what Sepultura learned from
reduction. Fast music can still leave distinct edges, allowing anger to arrive as a series
of decisions rather than one continuous rush.

Elsewhere, the band tests how much variation that new grammar can hold. “Propaganda” uses
a more nervous, forward-driving figure, then opens holes around Max's vocal instead of
settling into a broad chant. “We Who Are Not as Others” does the opposite: its deliberate
repetition turns refusal into endurance. These tracks are not interchangeable examples of
groove. One keeps trying to escape its bar; the other makes remaining in place the point.

## Kaiowas opens the circle

The acoustic “Kaiowas,” recorded at Chepstow Castle, is not an interlude that suspends the
album's force. Layered hand percussion and acoustic guitars move emphasis among the
players; no distorted riff is needed to organise attention. Its circular construction
replaces the front-facing command of the electric songs with group motion.

The recording's open air is audible in its looser boundary. Hands, strings, and drum skins
do not arrive with the close, hard outline of the studio tracks. The players pass a pulse
rather than doubling one another, and the accelerating final passage grows through
participation instead of amplification. This is the album's clearest proof that collective
weight need not mean uniformity.

That shift matters because _Chaos A.D._ is interested in more than individual fury. “We
Who Are Not as Others” builds identity through stubborn repetition, while the New Model
Army cover “The Hunt” lets the band test its weight against a song not written in metal's
vocabulary. Different materials enter the record, but rhythm keeps them in the same
argument.

## When directness becomes a slogan

The album's compression sometimes reduces thought as well as music. “Biotech Is Godzilla”
is a furious miniature aimed at corporate and political power, but its caricature offers
little room for the institutional complexity behind the anger. “Manifest,” built around
the Carandiru prison massacre, draws moral force from specificity, yet its documentary
framing and blunt refrain do not develop the event beyond indictment.

Later tracks also confirm more often than surprise. “Nomad” and “Clenched Fist” have
powerful low-slung figures, but by the close their method is familiar: isolate the riff,
enlarge the drum pattern, make the chorus a communal blow. Consistency gives the album
identity while narrowing its second-half dramatic range.

“The Hunt” presents a different problem. Sepultura thickens New Model Army's post-punk
song effectively, but the adaptation is so compatible with the surrounding record that it
loses some of the friction a cover can create. Its direct pulse and compact refrain fit
the new Sepultura almost too neatly. By contrast, “Kaiowas” changes the album's terms
rather than merely proving that another song can survive them.

## The beat remains contested ground

_Chaos A.D._ changed Sepultura by asking what heaviness could do once speed stopped being
its main proof. The answer is audible in the space between guitar strokes, in Igor
Cavalera's ability to redirect a riff from below, and in vocals shaped for collective
recall.

The album also changes the scale at which a metal band can sound political. Its strongest
moments do not place a message on top of an otherwise neutral attack. “Territory” makes
division audible in its stops and contested accents; “Refuse/Resist” builds assembly into
the relationship between drums and voice. The songs work before every reference is known,
but knowing the references does not exhaust what the rhythms say.

Its politics are strongest when embedded in those relationships. Musicians listen, answer,
and align without becoming identical. The weaker slogans show the limit of compression;
the best grooves show its democratic possibility. Sepultura made fewer notes carry more
bodies, and turned the downbeat into contested ground.

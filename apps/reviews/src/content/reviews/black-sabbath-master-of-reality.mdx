## Weight before explanation

The first sound on _Master of Reality_ is not a guitar. It is Tony Iommi coughing after
taking a pull on a joint, a bit of studio reality that turns into the count-in for “Sweet
Leaf.” Then the band lands on a riff that seems almost too plain to sustain a song: a low
bend, a short descent, a pause. The force comes from the pause as much as the notes.
Geezer Butler thickens Iommi's bend, then moves around the guitar's edges, while Bill Ward
places cymbal strikes and fills where a less alert drummer might settle for a square
backbeat.

Low tuning gives the album its register, but Ward and Butler determine how that register
moves. Sabbath treats a riff as a floor plan. The drums decide how quickly the listener
crosses it; the bass opens side passages; Ozzy Osbourne's melody traces the main wall in
clear, singable lines.

“Sweet Leaf” also states a recurring tension. The music sounds severe, but the band is not
solemn about its materials. The cough is funny. The lyric is direct enough to border on a
novelty song. The performance refuses novelty because the central riff has real
consequence: each return feels heavier after the brief, faster middle section. Speed is
not the destination. It is a change in pressure that sends the song back to its main
figure with renewed force.

## A new low register

“After Forever” complicates the album's reputation for monolithic darkness. Butler's lyric
addresses belief and doubt in blunt questions, while the music moves through more sections
than the opening track. Iommi alternates compact chord shapes with a brighter, almost
impatient figure. Ward follows each shift rather than flattening them into a single
groove. The result is argumentative: the song keeps changing its footing while the vocal
asks where conviction comes from.

The directness can be awkward. Lines land as declarations where another writer might leave
ambiguity. But that lack of polish fits the arrangement. Sabbath is not building a mist
around the subject. The band sets a question against a riff and lets both stand in hard
outline. Osbourne's doubled vocal helps. Its narrow melodic range makes the words feel
communal rather than privately confessional, as if a room is testing the questions
together.

The song also shows how economical the production is. Guitar overdubs add emphasis, but
they do not turn the riff into a wall of indistinct sound. Bass, guitar, and drums retain
separate contours. That separation is crucial on an album built from low frequencies.
Without it, heaviness would collapse into blur.

## Four tests of the method

### Sweet Leaf: Weight begins in the pause

The cough is Tony Iommi's, caught on tape after he inhaled from a joint; Rhino's account
confirms that the band kept it deliberately because it introduces a song about marijuana.
The edit turns breath, cough, and count-in into meter before the riff arrives.

The main figure contains a low bend, a short fall, and an empty beat. Ward does not fill
that gap mechanically. He varies the cymbal weight and lets his kick answer the guitar,
while Butler alternates between doubling Iommi and pushing past him. The faster middle
passage briefly removes the drag. When the opening riff returns, it feels heavier because
the band has changed the listener's internal tempo.

### Children of the Grave: Low tuning learns to run

Iommi and Butler tuned down three semitones for this album. On “Children of the Grave,”
Ward's rolling tom pattern gives that low register forward thrust. The guitar can sustain
thick, repeated chords without stopping the track because the drums keep pulling the bar
ahead.

Osbourne's long, narrow vocal line moves differently from the instruments beneath it. He
does not try to match every accent, so the warning remains intelligible while the band
churns. In the closing section, overdubbed percussion and whispers drain the charge rather
than supplying a larger climax. The coda is effective as an afterimage, though it is less
developed than the main performance.

### Lord of This World: The rhythm section tilts the mass

The opening riff lands behind the beat and risks becoming inert. Butler prevents that by
letting the bass scrape around the guitar's contour instead of copying it throughout. Ward
uses compact fills at the ends of phrases, shifting the center of gravity just before the
figure repeats. Much of the movement is below Osbourne's plain vocal line.

During Iommi's solo, the rhythm guitar keeps the low figure present and the bass continues
to argue with it. A small amount of material stays active because the four parts do not
agree on exactly how it should sit.

### Into the Void: The riff becomes an environment

“Into the Void” delays its verse through a sequence of distinct introductions. The first
figure crawls; the next adds lift; a stop-start passage narrows the motion before the
vocal enters. The riffs form a sequence, each teaching the listener a different gait
inside the same low-tuned register.

Once Osbourne arrives, his melody closely shadows the guitar. Voice and riff seem to
occupy one object, appropriate for a lyric about escaping a damaged world without any
convincing sense of escape in the music. The final returns draw more from persistence than
transformation. That is the song's limit, but Ward and Butler keep the repeated surface
alive long enough for the environment to hold.

## What economy makes possible

The album's decisive strength is proportion. Ward and Butler keep Iommi's lowered guitar
from becoming a static wall. Their parts make “Sweet Leaf” lurch, “Children of the Grave”
run, and “Lord of This World” lean backward even though all three depend on compact,
repeated figures.

“Embryo” and “Orchid” also perform real sequencing work. Their acoustic attacks decay
quickly and reset the ear before the amplified low end returns. The interludes are small,
but the album understands where to place them.

## Where austerity becomes repetition

Those instrumentals are slight when removed from the sequence. “Embryo” barely has time to
state its figure, and “Orchid” develops touch more than form. They function better as
editing decisions than as complete compositions.

“Solitude” reveals a different constraint. The exposed voice and flute add vulnerability,
but the arrangement establishes its lament early and then sustains it. Osbourne's direct
melodies often help the riffs read clearly; here that plainness leaves little room for the
mood to change.

## The riff as a complete world

_Master of Reality_ made lower tuning useful because the band knew what to do around it.
Empty beats, moving bass, and drums that refuse a square backbeat give the riffs different
kinds of weight. The album's smaller pieces depend heavily on placement, and its longest
returns sometimes outlast their development. Here, heaviness comes from the relationship
between mass and room.

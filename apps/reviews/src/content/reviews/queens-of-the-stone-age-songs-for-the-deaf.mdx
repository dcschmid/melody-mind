<h2 id="violent-tuning">Violent tuning</h2>

_Songs for the Deaf_ begins by searching the radio. Voices and fragments pass until a host
promises music for a drive through the desert. Then “You Think I Ain't Worth a Dollar, but
I Feel Like a Millionaire” arrives as if the station has overloaded the speakers. Nick
Oliveri's scream is clipped and hostile; guitar and bass repeat a blunt figure; Dave
Grohl's drums make the repetition feel like acceleration.

The radio device is more than a joke about bad programming. It removes the clean frame
around the album. Songs arrive through compression, chatter, and somebody else's sequence.
Queens of the Stone Age build a controlled studio record and then pretend it has been
caught in unreliable transmission.

That tension keeps the road concept from becoming scenery. The listener does not gaze at
desert space. The listener is trapped with a dial, an engine, and changing signals. The
opening song is short because it functions as impact after tuning. It leaves before the
chosen station can stop feeling dangerous.

<h2 id="moving-machine">A moving machine</h2>

“No One Knows” is built on a guitar riff simple enough to expose every rhythmic decision
around it. Josh Homme's figure has a clipped, almost comic bounce. Grohl refuses to treat
it as a fixed loop. Ghost notes, tom movement, and changing cymbal weight make each return
lean differently.

The chorus opens the vocal melody without abandoning the riff's dry character. Homme sings
with a smoothness that does not compete with the drums. His restraint lets the track
contain swagger without turning into a display of toughness. The machine moves because
nobody is trying to sound larger than it.

“First It Giveth” tightens the exchange. The verse pushes forward in narrow space; the
chorus adds height but not comfort. Mark Lanegan's backing presence darkens the vocal
field, one example of how the album uses multiple singers as changes in weather. Homme,
Oliveri, and Lanegan do not perform interchangeable frontman roles. Each changes what the
same low guitar language can imply.

“The Sky Is Fallin'” slows the motion and lets the guitars stretch. Its size is useful
after three compact attacks, though the repeated chorus carries less information than the
arrangement around it. The album's road can sometimes make persistence seem like
development. Here atmosphere does part of the work the song form leaves unfinished.

<h2 id="drum-architecture">Drum architecture</h2>

“A Song for the Dead” belongs to Grohl as much as to its writers. The opening rolls do not
merely introduce the riff; they define the track's entire arc. He moves between forward
charge, sudden space, and fills that reset the band's weight. Guitar repetition becomes
dramatic because the drums keep changing its scale.

Lanegan's low vocal makes the song feel inevitable rather than frantic. That contrast is
the key. The band moves at high pressure while the singer sounds as if he has already seen
the destination. When the final section appears to stop, Grohl restarts the engine with a
fill so direct that the false ending becomes physical comedy and renewed threat at once.

“The Hanging Tree” uses a tighter, stranger pulse. Lanegan again brings gravity, but the
guitar figure circles instead of charging. “Go With the Flow” then strips the record to
bright forward motion. Its repeated piano note and driving drums create a pop clarity the
album has mostly withheld. The song works because the sequence has earned simplicity.

“Gonna Leave You” and “Do It Again” continue the drive with less distinction. Oliveri's
melodic vocal changes his role effectively, and the latter track has an insistent groove,
but both depend on an engine the album has already demonstrated in more revealing forms.
They are road, not landmark.

<h2 id="abrasion-and-space">Abrasion and space</h2>

“God Is in the Radio” justifies the concept by making broadcast language part of the
song's slow hypnosis. Lanegan's voice, Homme's guitar leads, and a patient rhythm section
create a late-night stretch where time loosens. The performance does not rush to explain
its title. It lets repetition become reception: a phrase heard often enough starts to
sound like belief.

“Another Love Song” is deliberately lighter and almost sour in its tunefulness. The change
of singer and harmonic color prevents the final third from becoming uniformly dark. Yet
the skits around this part of the record begin to show their age. Their abrasive humor
establishes character on first contact; repeated listening turns some of it into delay.

<h2 id="refused-destination">The destination refuses resolution</h2>

“Song for the Deaf” gathers the album's voices into its most oppressive space. The guitar
is broad and dragging, the drums strike with less bounce, and Lanegan's vocal sounds
embedded in the low register. A hidden coda and continued radio interference deny the
clean ending a concept album might demand.

“Mosquito Song” provides the acoustic afterimage. Strings, guitar, and macabre imagery
replace engine force with a carefully arranged miniature. It is not a moral to the drive.
It is what remains after the station fades: craft, appetite, and threat at room volume.
The original CD also hides “The Real Song for the Deaf” in the pregap before track one.
Its low-frequency joke and spoken announcement belong to the radio frame rather than the
main run of fourteen compositions, but they make the listener search for the album before
the opening station can be found.

<h2 id="complete-sequence">Fourteen songs and a hidden frequency</h2>

“You Think I Ain't Worth a Dollar, but I Feel Like a Millionaire” begins with a radio
voice and then overloads it, Nick Oliveri's scream turning the first proper song into a
blown speaker. “No One Knows” is more spacious. Its familiar riff matters because Dave
Grohl's drums keep changing the force around it. “First It Giveth” tightens the pattern
until its brief retreat makes the final return hit harder. “A Song for the Dead” stretches
the opening run into a drum-led piece whose stops feel dangerous because Grohl keeps
carrying momentum into them.

“The Sky Is Fallin'” slows the vehicle and lets the guitars widen. “Six Shooter” rejects
that room in ninety seconds of Oliveri-led abrasion; it works as a jolt, not as a
developed song. “Hanging Tree” puts Mark Lanegan over a circling guitar figure, creating
tension without another sprint. “Go With the Flow” answers with the record's cleanest
forward line, then “Gonna Leave You” changes Oliveri from a scream to a dry melodic lead.

“Do It Again” is intentionally repetitive, but its place exposes the album's middle limit:
propulsion can substitute for a new idea. “God Is in the Radio” restores depth through
Lanegan's low vocal and patient guitar leads. “Another Love Song” uses a sour, lighter
melody to break that nocturnal spell without pretending the drive has become pleasant.

“Song for the Deaf” is the heavy terminus, broad and slow enough to make the earlier speed
feel distant. “Mosquito Song” then steps outside the electric system with acoustic guitar,
strings, and brass. The sequence does not arrive at a destination. It changes scale until
the road can be heard at room volume.

The album's central trick survives its overlong broadcast jokes because the musical system
holds. Repetition establishes geography. Different singers change perspective. Drummers
change suspension and speed. _Songs for the Deaf_ reaches its limits when road texture
substitutes for a new turn, but its best tracks make the drive itself a form. There is no
destination that could improve the machine.

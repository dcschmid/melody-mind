## A road trip built from interruption

_Songs for the Deaf_ begins by searching the radio. Voices and fragments pass until a host
promises music for a drive through the desert. Then “You Think I Ain't Worth a Dollar, but
I Feel Like a Millionaire” arrives as if the station has overloaded the speakers. Nick
Oliveri's scream is clipped and hostile; guitar and bass repeat a blunt figure; Dave
Grohl's drums make the repetition feel like acceleration.

The radio device is more than a joke about bad programming. It removes the clean frame
around the album. Songs arrive through compression, chatter, and somebody else's sequence.
Queens of the Stone Age build a controlled studio record and then pretend it has been
caught in unreliable transmission.

That tension keeps the road concept from becoming scenery. The listener does not gaze at
desert space. The listener is trapped with a dial, an engine, and changing signals. The
opening song is short because it functions as impact after tuning. It leaves before the
chosen station can stop feeling dangerous.

## The radio as structural glue

“No One Knows” is built on a guitar riff simple enough to expose every rhythmic decision
around it. Josh Homme's figure has a clipped, almost comic bounce. Grohl refuses to treat
it as a fixed loop. Ghost notes, tom movement, and changing cymbal weight make each return
lean differently.

The chorus opens the vocal melody without abandoning the riff's dry character. Homme sings
with a smoothness that does not compete with the drums. His restraint lets the track
contain swagger without turning into a display of toughness. The machine moves because
nobody is trying to sound larger than it.

“First It Giveth” tightens the exchange. The verse pushes forward in narrow space; the
chorus adds height but not comfort. Mark Lanegan's backing presence darkens the vocal
field, one example of how the album uses multiple singers as changes in weather. Homme,
Oliveri, and Lanegan do not perform interchangeable frontman roles. Each changes what the
same low guitar language can imply.

“The Sky Is Fallin'” slows the motion and lets the guitars stretch. Its size is useful
after three compact attacks, though the repeated chorus carries less information than the
arrangement around it. The album's road can sometimes make persistence seem like
development. Here atmosphere does part of the work the song form leaves unfinished.

## Four stops along the drive

### You Think I Ain't Worth a Dollar, but I Feel Like a Millionaire: The radio overloads

A mock station voice promises desert programming before Oliveri's clipped scream and a
blunt riff seem to overload the broadcast. The transition turns studio compression into
part of the fiction: the song does not merely follow the DJ; it sounds too hot for the
channel that introduced it.

The drums create acceleration inside a figure that changes very little. The kick thickens
the guitar, short fills shove the phrase forward, and the track leaves before the impact
can settle into routine. It is an efficient opening because the road begins as a problem
of reception, not scenery.

### No One Knows: Drums reorganize the riff

Homme's guitar figure has a clipped bounce and enough empty space to reveal every decision
around it. Grohl changes ghost notes, tom movement, and cymbal weight across its returns.
The riff remains recognizable while its footing keeps shifting. Bass reinforces the low
shape without sealing those gaps.

Homme sings smoothly and slightly behind the instrumental attack. That restraint prevents
the song's swagger from becoming a contest for size. The chorus opens the melody, but the
rhythm section preserves the dry mechanical oddness of the verse. Polish clarifies the
machine instead of hiding it.

### Song for the Dead: Two speeds occupy one performance

Grohl's opening rolls establish the track before the guitars fully arrive. Throughout the
song, his fills do not decorate stops; they decide whether a stop feels final, comic, or
threatening. The restart near the end lands because the drummer has controlled the
listener's sense of momentum from the first bar.

Lanegan's low delivery creates a second speed. The band charges while the singer sounds as
if the destination is already known. That separation gives repeated guitar figures a
dramatic arc they would not possess alone. Velocity and inevitability coexist rather than
taking turns.

### God Is in the Radio: Patience changes the road

Late in the sequence, Lanegan's low vocal and Homme's measured lead guitar open a darker,
less directive space. Notes are allowed to decay, and the rhythm section waits before
turning each rise into impact. The drive continues, but the sensation changes from speed
to the altered attention of a long night journey.

Here the broadcast concept stops functioning as a run of jokes and becomes a way of
hearing. Repeated phrases can resemble belief when the source is invisible and the signal
keeps returning. The track does not explain its title; its patient form lets transmission,
repetition, and suggestion do the work.

## Repetition stays mobile

The record makes repetition mobile. Homme's economical riffs leave room for drums to
change their footing, and Grohl's performances on the central run give stops and restarts
unusual dramatic force. The rotating singers change the same guitar language more than an
additional layer of production could: Oliveri makes it volatile, Homme dry and controlled,
Lanegan inevitable.

The radio interludes also damage the clean border around each song. Compression, chatter,
and false station identities make the sequence feel received under unreliable conditions.
That frame converts an eclectic set into one interrupted drive.

## Where the road flattens

The station jokes lose information quickly. Their abrasion establishes character on first
contact, but repeated listening turns several interruptions into fixed delays. The device
is strongest when it affects the sound of an entrance and weakest when it only supplies a
punch line.

The final third also contains songs that keep the engine running without changing it. “Do
It Again” has an effective groove, and “Another Love Song” changes harmonic color, but
neither redirects the album as sharply as “God Is in the Radio” or “Song for the Deaf.”

## The signal cuts out

_Songs for the Deaf_ reproduces the unstable attention of a long drive. Repeated guitar
figures provide the road; singers, drummers, and changing signals alter what that road
feels like. The broadcast frame occasionally overstays its function, and not every late
song finds a new gear. Its best performances keep discovering different pressure inside
limited material.

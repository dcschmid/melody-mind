## Weight that will not hold still

“Let Me Drown” begins with a riff that promises solid ground. Kim Thayil and Chris Cornell
push broad guitar chords against Ben Shepherd's bass, while Matt Cameron gives the song a
forceful forward motion. Cornell's melody does something less stable. It rises over the
riff, pulls away from its accents, and turns physical release into an image of erasure.

That disagreement runs through _Superunknown_. Soundgarden remained a heavy band, but
weight no longer carried one reliable meaning. Lowered and altered tunings can suggest
power, illness, suspension, or exhaustion. Irregular meters disturb a groove without
announcing a technical exercise. Cornell's voice can dominate an arrangement and still
sound trapped inside it.

The album expands by making its materials uncertain. A riff is not a verdict. It is an
environment whose emotional gravity can change as the band moves through it.

## Four writers, one unsettled scale

Soundgarden recorded their fourth album after the touring cycle for _Badmotorfinger_. The
band worked with producer Michael Beinhorn, recorded primarily at Bad Animals Studio in
Seattle, and mixed the album with Brendan O'Brien. The standard edition contains fifteen
tracks and closes with “Like Suicide.” “She Likes Surprises,” included on some editions
and current services, sits outside the sequence considered here.

Writing came from across the group. Cornell, Thayil, Shepherd, and Cameron brought songs
with different internal logics, while the arrangements retained a shared interest in
altered tuning and displaced pulse. The variety is structural. Shepherd's “Head Down”
bends around a loose, droning guitar figure; Cameron's “Fresh Tendrils,” co-written with
Cornell, advances through carefully placed rhythmic tension; Cornell's “Black Hole Sun”
opens harmonic space that the band's earlier attack might have filled.

The production enlarges these differences. Guitars are layered, yet individual textures
remain identifiable. Bass can carry melodic movement below a fixed guitar shape. Drums
have enough depth to make odd groupings feel bodily. Cornell is clearly in front without
being detached from the pressure behind him.

## Three forms of unstable gravity

### My Wave: Irregular time becomes ease

“My Wave” organizes much of its main riff in five beats, but Cameron avoids making the
meter sound counted. His backbeat and the band's repeated accents turn asymmetry into a
rolling motion. Shepherd keeps the low end direct, while the guitars leave short spaces
around the riff rather than sustaining a continuous wall.

Cornell's melody treats that uneven ground as freedom. He stretches simple phrases across
the pulse and delivers the refrain with relaxed authority. The lyric asks for personal
space; the meter gives that request physical form by refusing the expected path without
losing its groove. Technical difference becomes posture.

### The Day I Tried to Live: Seven beats resist recovery

The central guitar movement descends in half steps and unfolds across an asymmetrical
meter. Each return sounds familiar but slightly delayed. Cameron makes the seven-beat
shape legible through repeated placement instead of busy fills, while Shepherd's bass adds
a lower contour that prevents the riff from feeling static.

Cornell sings from the aftermath of an attempted change. His long lines rise above the
descending guitar, creating a brief impression of escape before the riff pulls the song
back. The chorus grows wider without becoming triumphant. Form and lyric agree on the
difficulty of breaking a pattern, but the music avoids reducing that difficulty to defeat.

### 4th of July: Low tuning becomes atmosphere

“4th of July” moves at a pace that makes each guitar attack feel separate. The tuning
drops the riff into a blurred low register; Thayil's lead sounds hover above it like heat
distortion. Cameron resists driving the song faster, placing cymbal and snare accents far
enough apart for decay to become part of the rhythm.

Cornell uses a lower, less declarative delivery than on the album's larger choruses. The
apocalyptic images remain partly abstract, but the arrangement supplies their physical
weather. Heaviness here is neither aggression nor release. It is duration: the sense that
the air has become difficult to move through.

## Meter follows emotional pressure

Cameron's drumming is essential to the album's range. He can make five or seven beats feel
conversational because he identifies the song's larger motion instead of emphasizing every
irregular division. “Spoonman” switches its grouping while retaining a danceable thrust;
“Fresh Tendrils” uses accents to make tension gather gradually. The meters alter balance,
not the band's credibility as a rock rhythm section.

The altered tunings perform a related task. They change the relationship between an open
string, a fretted chord, and Cornell's melody. On “Mailman,” low guitar weight turns the
vocal's patience into menace. “Black Hole Sun” uses a more open, circling progression;
Cornell's melody can sound inviting even as the arrangement becomes unreal. The songs do
not share one grunge texture. Each establishes its own center of gravity.

Cornell's range adds another source of instability. His high notes can cut through dense
guitars, but his quieter phrasing often carries more uncertainty. The album uses vocal
command to ask whether command has practical value inside these environments.

## Seventy minutes of dark weather

The standard sequence is long enough for its middle-tempo weight to lose distinction.
“Limo Wreck,” “Fresh Tendrils,” and “4th of July” each build a separate rhythmic and tonal
world, but their placement across the second half can make heaviness feel like a general
climate. The short “Kickstand” provides one release; more changes of scale might have made
the surrounding songs register more sharply.

Cornell's lyrics also vary in precision. “Fell on Black Days” turns a plain phrase into a
careful account of dread that arrives without a visible cause. Elsewhere, images of
poison, death, and damaged perception accumulate faster than the speakers acquire context.
The arrangements can be exact about how distress feels while the words leave its
circumstances unlocated.

That abstraction is sometimes the point, especially on “4th of July.” Across a seventy-
minute record, however, recurring darkness can connect songs the music has worked hard to
differentiate.

## The riff remains an open question

_Superunknown_ does not abandon Soundgarden's weight. It subjects weight to more uses.
Alternate tunings give each song a different floor, Cameron's meters alter the listener's
balance, and Cornell's melodies refuse to accept the riff's first emotional answer.

The long sequence occasionally turns those distinctions into weather. Some lyrics rely on
dark images where the music offers more exact information. Three songs resist that blur
especially well. “My Wave” makes asymmetry feel loose, “The Day I Tried to Live” makes
repetition feel contested, and “4th of July” makes low tuning feel environmental.

Heaviness becomes a question about space, time, and perspective. Soundgarden can still
strike with enormous force, but force no longer tells the listener what the song means.

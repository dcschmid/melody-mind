## The challenge arrives before the music

_Horses_ begins with a person claiming the room before the band has established a song.
Patti Smith's first sentence is delivered almost alone, at conversational speed, with no
melodic display to soften its refusal. The entrance matters because it defines the album's
working relationship. Voice is not one layer waiting to be balanced against guitar, piano,
bass, and drums. It is the event to which those instruments must respond.

That relationship had been developed in poetry readings and New York club performances
before John Cale produced the album at Electric Lady Studios. Lenny Kaye, Ivan Král,
Richard Sohl, and Jay Dee Daugherty do not treat Smith as a reciter who happens to have a
rock group behind her. They listen for pressure inside her phrasing: a repeated word, an
accelerated clause, a pause that might become a count-in. The songs gain form as the band
recognizes those signals and turns them into riffs, crescendos, and changes of gait.

This is the album's durable break with decorous literary rock. Its achievement is not a
prestige mixture of poetry and music. _Horses_ makes language a practical problem for a
band, then lets the process of solving it remain audible.

## Borrowed songs become contested ground

Two of the album's largest pieces contain earlier rock songs: Them's “Gloria” and Chris
Kenner's “Land of a Thousand Dances.” Smith neither hides those sources nor approaches
them with museum respect. Familiar material gives the listener a stable outline, which she
can occupy, interrupt, and redirect. Authorship becomes something performed rather than
settled by the writing credit.

### Gloria: A cover reclaimed in stages

“Gloria” joins Smith's poem “Oath” to the garage-rock standard. At first, the borrowed
song is barely present. Her dry, close vocal establishes its own pace while guitar answers
with small figures instead of announcing the famous riff at full weight. Piano, bass, and
drums enter by degrees. The three-chord cycle eventually becomes unmistakable, but by then
Smith has changed what its arrival means.

The performance does not reverse the original narrative with one lyrical substitution. It
redistributes authority over time. Smith moves from speech into pitch, from private
address into group chant, and the band grows more forceful as her diction becomes more
repetitive. When the song reaches its familiar spelled-out refrain, recognition feels less
like a return to the source than the completion of a takeover. A standard built for
directness has been made to contain hesitation, argument, and self-invention.

## A band learns to move with speech

The rhythm section is often described as primitive because it avoids ornamental playing.
That misses its harder task. The musicians must be steady enough to give Smith resistance
and flexible enough to follow a sentence that may lengthen without warning. Their economy
keeps the voice legible while small changes in density tell the listener how far a passage
has travelled.

### Birdland: Narration teaches the band to rise

“Birdland,” inspired by Peter Reich's account of his father, begins around Richard Sohl's
circling piano and Smith's patient narration. There is no conventional verse whose return
promises orientation. Instead, guitar color and cymbal activity accumulate as the boy's
vision becomes more urgent. Smith shortens the distance between spoken rhythm and singing:
syllables stretch, pitches climb, and repeated phrases cease to explain the scene and
start to enact it.

The band follows that transformation without illustrating every image. Daugherty increases
the physical insistence, Kaye and Král thicken the harmony, and Sohl's figure changes from
setting to propulsion. The long ascent works because no single cue starts it. Voice and
ensemble keep handing momentum to one another until narration has become collective
motion.

## Short songs alter the pressure

The tighter pieces prevent openness from becoming the album's only method. “Free Money”
drives a fantasy of escape with bright piano and increasingly impatient drums. “Kimberly”
sets protective language against a compact rock arrangement, while “Break It Up” uses a
slower, more deliberate rise. These songs prove that Smith does not need nine minutes to
disturb a familiar form.

### Redondo Beach: A light groove refuses the story

“Redondo Beach” makes the sharpest contrast. Offbeat guitar, mobile bass, and an easy
reggae-derived pulse create social lightness beneath a search that ends in death. Smith
does not sing the story as a continuous lament. Her clipped lines sit inside the groove,
and the refrain returns with a calm that grows more troubling each time.

The arrangement refuses to certify the lyric's emotion. That separation gives the song its
force: tragedy is not announced by a minor-key collapse, and the band does not abandon its
poise to prove that the words matter. The listener has to hold incompatible signals at
once. Here the ensemble is not following Smith's rising intensity; it is supplying the
distance against which her account becomes credible.

## Land risks losing its boundary

### Land: A suite held together by return

“Land” is where the album's method becomes most exposed. A spoken scene around the figure
of Johnny moves through incantation, violent interruption, the dance command of “Land of a
Thousand Dances,” and echoes of language heard earlier on the record. The band repeatedly
finds a pulse, pushes it toward frenzy, and lets Smith break its frame. Return rather than
plot holds the suite together: gestures recur changed by what has happened between them.

The scale permits a remarkable transition from private vision to communal rock ritual. It
also reveals the cost of the album's freedom. Once the central mechanism is understood,
some later surges confirm intensity rather than redirect it. The performance remains
alive, but duration occasionally substitutes for a new dramatic decision. “Elegie,” sparse
and mournful after that excess, closes the record with atmosphere more than resolution.
Its brevity is welcome, though it leaves several of the album's tensions deliberately
open.

## Authorship stays in motion

_Horses_ did not need to invent rock vocabulary to change who could command it. Its basic
materials—three-chord songs, backbeats, piano figures, repeated refrains—were already worn
smooth. Smith and the band make them unstable by changing the chain of command. Sometimes
the voice sets the pace; sometimes a groove contradicts the words; sometimes an inherited
song provides the wall that Smith pushes against.

The looser passages do not all justify their length equally, and the compact songs can
make the band's language sound more conventional. Those limits belong to the same wager as
the album's strengths. _Horses_ lets form emerge from attention among musicians rather
than from a fixed separation between poem, singer, and accompaniment. What lasts is not
the claim that poetry can belong in rock. It is the sound of a band discovering that the
poem has already changed the beat.

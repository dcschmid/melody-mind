The first mistake is to listen only for Fela Kuti. His tenor saxophone, electric
piano, and voice command attention, but the music does not begin with a solitary
figure waiting for accompaniment. It begins with a group learning how to hold several
short parts at once. A guitar repeats one dry phrase. Another guitar answers from a
different point in the bar. Bass refuses to decorate the harmony and instead makes a
small pattern feel permanent. Percussion divides the pulse again. Tony Allen’s drum
kit moves among those lines without flattening them into a single backbeat.

When the horns finally enter, they do not rescue an empty arrangement. They strike
across a machine already in motion. That is the practical achievement of Africa 70:
the band made repetition sound less like stasis than collective concentration. Fela
could extend a piece past ten minutes because the arrangement was not a verse waiting
for a chorus. It was a set of relationships that could absorb speech, solos, choral
answers, and political pressure without losing its center. [1](#source-veal-analysis)

## Before the name settled

Fela Ransome-Kuti studied music in London, where he formed Koola Lobitos and worked
within jazz and highlife. After returning to Nigeria, he rebuilt the group. The names
changed—Koola Lobitos, Nigeria 70, Africa 70, and later Afrika 70—but the important
continuity was rehearsal. The music that came to be called Afrobeat was not produced
by placing a ready-made African rhythm under American funk. It developed through a
Lagos band testing how highlife, jazz improvisation, Yoruba rhythmic knowledge, soul,
and funk could occupy the same long form. [2](#source-official-about)

The group’s 1969 tour of the United States sharpened Fela’s political language. His
encounters with Black Power thought, including conversations with Sandra Izsadore,
changed the subjects and directness of his writing. The musical change was already a
collective problem. A new lyric needed a form that could speak in public without
turning the band into background. Call and response offered one answer; an ensemble
that could build tension for minutes before the vocal arrived offered another.

Afrobeat’s familiar ingredients can be listed quickly. The list explains little. The
sound depends on allocation: which instrument owns which subdivision, which phrase is
allowed to repeat, and who signals the next layer. A large band works only if each
musician accepts that a small part can carry structural weight.

## One drummer, several directions

Tony Allen had played jazz and highlife before becoming the drummer at the center of
Fela’s bands. His kit did not copy the percussion section. Hands and feet maintained
different lines, leaving openings where a conventional rock or funk drummer might
place a heavy statement. Snare accents could converse with guitar rather than merely
confirm beats two and four. The bass drum did not have to shadow every bass-guitar
attack. Hi-hat and cymbal patterns supplied motion without turning the groove into a
straight grid.

Allen described Fela’s compositions as challenges. On “Question Jam Answer,” he heard
the writing as an invitation to find a drum pattern that was neither common nor easy
to replace. When illness kept him away, substitute drummers struggled to make the
band function. The anecdote is not proof that one musician invented the whole style.
It shows how deeply a personal drum language had entered the arrangement. Fela later
said Allen played like four drummers; the official archive identifies his patterns as
signature ingredients across fourteen years of collaboration. [3](#source-allen-guardian)

This complexity did not require constant display. Allen’s playing often sounds calm
because independence is distributed across limbs. No single gesture has to announce
the difficulty. The listener hears continuity while the band hears several reference
points. That difference helped Fela write music whose scale could grow without a
constant increase in volume.

## Guitar as measured labor

The guitars supply another lesson in restraint. In many Africa 70 recordings, two
players repeat different single-note or lightly voiced figures. Their parts interlock,
but they are not interchangeable. One may stress the offbeat while the other places a
short answer around it. The result is a rhythmic surface with no obvious seam.

This work is easy to undervalue because the phrases rarely develop like featured
solos. Repetition makes every uneven attack audible. The player has to maintain tone,
placement, and length while drums, percussion, and voices alter the surrounding
weight. A slightly longer note can crowd another part. A stronger accent can make the
whole bar lean incorrectly.

Fela’s authorship included assigning and policing these cells. That authority should
not erase the musicians who gave them feel. Africa 70 was both a band and an
organization with a demanding leader. Its sound came from disciplined collective
labor, while credit and money remained sources of tension. Allen’s eventual departure
cannot be reduced to one clean dispute; his own accounts describe exhaustion,
production delays, and the need for room beyond Fela’s organization.

## The horns do not float above the rhythm

Africa 70’s horn lines often arrive as blocks: compact riffs, held notes, or short
responses to Fela’s call. Jazz training remains audible in the voicings and solos, but
the section’s main structural job is rhythmic. Horns can announce a threshold, tighten
the groove, or withdraw so that the next entrance carries force.

Fela frequently delayed his own vocal. An instrumental opening could establish each
layer before the song declared its argument. This changed the meaning of length. The
record was not extended because nobody edited it. Duration let the audience learn the
system bodily. By the time Fela introduced a subject, the band had already established
the terms of attention.

Allen resisted radio edits because, in his view, repeated cutting killed the music.
Afrobeat needed time to take hold. His comment is more precise than the romantic idea
of an endless African groove. These pieces are arranged. Entrances, breaks, horn
responses, solos, and vocal sections happen in a controlled order. Length makes those
changes legible against a stable field. [4](#source-allen-guardian)

## The Shrine as a testing room

Fela’s club in Lagos, known as the Afrika Shrine, was not simply a venue where finished
records were reproduced. Regular performances let the band live with new material in
front of an audience. A composition could change between its first rehearsals and its
studio version. The room joined musical work to Fela’s public speech, dance, ritual,
and confrontation with state authority.

That continuity matters. A band rehearsing the same cells night after night can alter
weight without rewriting the chart. A horn cue can become later. A guitar figure can
grow drier. A vocal call can wait until the crowd understands its answer. The Shrine
made arrangement an ongoing practice rather than a document completed at the desk.

It also concentrated risk. Police harassment, arrests, and raids followed Fela’s
provocations and open criticism of Nigeria’s military governments. The club and the
Kalakuta Republic compound made his politics materially visible. Musicians, dancers,
family members, workers, and residents lived with consequences that a heroic portrait
focused on Fela alone can obscure. [5](#source-official-about)

## The answer changes the speaker

The chorus did more than reinforce a hook. Fela often delivered a line in Nigerian
Pidgin and received a compact sung answer. That exchange redistributed the song’s
argument. A statement from the bandleader became something a group could confirm,
mock, or return. Onstage, dancers and singers made the response visible as well as
audible, joining movement to the rhythm section’s repeated cells.

This role deserves care because the women around Fela have often been collapsed into
his image. They were performers and workers whose voices, choreography, clothes, and
presence helped define the Shrine, while their individual histories received less
documentation than his. Calling the ensemble collective cannot erase that imbalance.
The records nevertheless show a precise musical fact: remove the answer and Fela’s
call changes character. It becomes a speech delivered at the band rather than a phrase
whose timing depends on other voices.

Call and response also helped long texts remain musical. Fela could move from a
specific accusation into a recurring phrase that listeners already knew how to enter.
The chorus did not summarize the verse. It supplied a return point, as structural as a
horn riff or bass pattern.

## Recording without shrinking the band

Studio work presented a different problem: how to keep the band’s internal depth when
microphones and tape could turn a large room into a crowded signal. Fela usually
produced his own albums and preferred to record in Lagos. The official archive notes
only a small number of outside producers, among them Jeff Jarratt, Ginger Baker,
Dennis Bovell, and Wally Badarou. This was control, not isolation. Engineers and
producers still shaped sessions, but they entered an aesthetic whose balance Fela
guarded closely. [6](#source-official-collaborations)

The best recordings allow the rhythm section to remain separable. Bass is firm without
swallowing guitar. Allen’s kit has detail across snare, cymbal, toms, and bass drum.
Horns can be thick without making the percussion disappear. Studio Oral History
accounts emphasize preparation: material changed between composition, rehearsal, and
recording as parts were added or removed. Tape captured the result of that process,
not an untouched live instinct. [7](#source-tape-op)

“Zombie” demonstrates the method’s economy. The band locks into a severe march-like
figure, while Fela’s commands and the chorus’s answers parody military obedience. The
arrangement does not illustrate the lyric from outside; its repetitions make the
discipline under attack physically present. Horn punches and vocal orders become part
of the same mechanism.

## A system with unequal power

Calling Africa 70 a system must not make its people sound like components. Fela held
unusual control as composer, bandleader, employer, producer, political figure, and
owner of the public narrative. The musicians supplied individual knowledge that could
not simply be replaced. Those facts coexisted uneasily.

Fela recognized Allen’s value by producing records led by the drummer, including
_Jealousy_, _Progress_, and _No Accommodation for Lagos_. Allen recalled the final of
those sessions taking months while Fela reconsidered solos and mixes. By late 1978 he
left the organization. Fela then used two drummers to cover parts that Allen had held
within one body. [8](#source-official-collaborations)

The split clarifies authorship rather than settling it. Fela wrote, directed, and
defined Afrobeat’s public purpose. Allen’s drum language made that purpose move.
Guitarists, bassists, percussionists, horn players, and singers maintained the network
between them. No fair account needs to choose between the bandleader and the band.

## Hear the part before the monument

Fela’s image has become monumental: raised fist, saxophone, briefs, smoke, speeches,
and defiance. The image carries real history, including violence inflicted by the
state. It can also reduce the records to a soundtrack for biography.

A different way into the work is to choose one short passage and follow one player.
Listen to the rhythm guitar without waiting for a solo. Notice whether its phrase
begins before or after the bass. Follow the hi-hat while the snare seems to argue with
it. Count how long the band plays before the first extended lyric, then notice what has
already changed. The arrangement becomes visible as labor.

Africa 70 could sound enormous because its members did not all make enormous gestures.
They accepted exact, repeated responsibilities and kept enough air between them for
Fela to redirect the form. The politics mattered because the words were dangerous and
public. The music carried them because a band had built a structure strong enough to
hold attention before a single slogan arrived.

The BOSS HM-2 offers no control labeled “Stockholm.” Its four knobs read Level,
Color Mix Low, Color Mix High, and Distortion. BOSS introduced the pedal in 1983 as a
compact route toward the sound of a loud British amplifier stack. Players in Sweden
later discovered that turning every control clockwise produced something less
representational: a compressed, abrasive band of lows, high mids, and fizz that could
fill the width of a mix. The setting became so identifiable that a mass-produced
Japanese pedal now serves as shorthand for a regional death-metal sound.
[1](#source-boss-product)

## A stack model that did not stay a model

Compact distortion pedals translate several jobs into a low-voltage circuit. They
raise signal level, clip the waveform, filter frequencies, and provide an output that
can drive an amplifier. The HM-2 was designed during an early-1980s market for
hard-rock and metal equipment, when “amp in a box” meant approximating the density of
a British stack without requiring its stage volume. BOSS manufactured the original
from 1983 to 1991, first in Japan and later in Taiwan. [1](#source-boss-product)

The enclosure follows BOSS’s durable standard: a hinged footswitch, recessed controls,
battery access under the treadle, buffered bypass, and color coding that lets a player
identify the model on a floor. The HM-2’s orange lettering and knob caps are visual
information, not decoration alone. Among similar black boxes, the color tells a
technician which circuit is about to enter the chain.

The control layout separates two frequency bands. “Color Mix” is not a conventional
three-band amplifier equalizer. Its low and high controls apply strong shaping at
different points in the spectrum, and their ranges overlap with the pedal’s clipping
character. Level sets output; Distortion changes gain. At moderate positions the
HM-2 can produce overdrive, mid-heavy distortion, or a thick bass response. The famous
setting ignores moderation.

Inside the circuit, buffered input and output stages surround gain, diode clipping,
and active filtering. The buffer controls how a guitar signal is presented to the
rest of a chain even when long cables and several pedals are present. Silicon diodes
constrain waveform peaks in a predictable range; filtering before and after clipping
determines which frequencies hit that limit hardest and which are emphasized
afterward. The order matters. Boosting a frequency into clipping does not produce the
same result as boosting it only at the output.

Original units also belong to an older BOSS power standard. Many were designed around
the ACA adapter system rather than today’s PSA nine-volt convention. Used alone on an
incorrect supply, an old pedal may receive less effective voltage and behave
differently; in a daisy chain with other grounded pedals, the result can change again.
That practical wrinkle is one reason comparisons between vintage pedals require more
than matching knob photographs.

## What “all knobs up” actually does

Turning Level fully clockwise sends a strong signal onward. Maximum Distortion drives
the clipping stages hard. Maximum Low and High do not create a neutral full-range
boost; they emphasize the pedal’s chosen bands, leaving the middle shaped by the
interaction of filters and clipping. The output is dense and noisy, with a pronounced
upper-mid grind and substantial low-frequency energy.

Descriptions such as “chainsaw” and “buzzsaw” capture texture but conceal frequency.
A saw metaphor suggests treble alone. The HM-2 setting feels large because low content
survives beneath the rasp. Multiple guitar tracks increase that spread. Pick attack
becomes a brief edge inside a more continuous block, especially at fast tempos.

The pedal is only one stage. Guitar pickups, tuning, strings, the receiving amplifier,
speaker, microphone, performance, and mix determine the record. Two musicians can put
four knobs at the same position and produce clearly different results. The setting is
a repeatable condition, not a finished sound.

Microphone choice and placement decide how much of the abrasive top survives. A close
dynamic microphone can focus the speaker’s midrange and reject part of the room; a
second microphone or guitar layer can restore body or attack. Engineers can filter
low frequencies that conflict with bass guitar and kick drum, then place the
recognizable grind above them. The finished “all knobs up” record may contain
substantial subtraction after the pedal’s maximum boosts.

This makes isolated demonstrations misleading in both directions. A single HM-2 into
a clean practice amplifier may sound thinner or harsher than a Sunlight record. A
carefully mixed modern clone can sound larger than an original pedal in a rehearsal
room. Neither test establishes which circuit is authentic. It establishes what the
rest of that test chain allowed.

## Nihilist brings the box into Sunlight

The route to Stockholm death metal ran through Nihilist, the band that preceded
Entombed. In BOSS’s interview history, Sunlight Studio producer Tomas Skogsberg recalls
that guitarist and bassist Leif Cuzner brought an HM-2 into the studio. Uffe Cederlund
and Alex Hellid continued developing the guitar arrangement, and Skogsberg treated the
pedal as part of a layered recording method. [2](#source-boss-history)

This account is more useful than a discovery myth. No one uncovered a secret switch
inside the HM-2. A player arrived with commercially available equipment, a group
recognized an extreme setting’s use, and a producer learned how to record it. Sunlight
Studio then gave connected bands a place where that knowledge could accumulate.

For Entombed’s _Left Hand Path_, the chainsaw layer did not stand alone. Skogsberg has
described a small Peavey Studio Pro 40 receiving the HM-2, with another guitar sound
made through a Marshall combo and a BOSS DS-1. Combining a heavily filtered layer with
a more conventional distortion helped preserve definition. If every audible guitar
frequency came from the HM-2 alone, the mix could lose attack and space.
[4](#source-sunlight-interview)

The arrangement also depended on performance. Tremolo-picked passages turn the
pedal’s sustained grind into continuous motion. Palm-muted rhythms make its low
response pulse. Open chords allow adjacent pitches and distortion products to rub
together. The same setting reacts differently to each.

## Dismember makes consistency a method

Dismember’s recordings established a related but distinct use of the circuit. Guitarist
David Blomqvist has described the HM-2 as consistent across the band’s work and central
to its signature. The group’s first album, _Like an Ever Flowing Stream_, was recorded
at Sunlight with Skogsberg, placing personnel, room, and equipment inside the same
regional exchange that shaped Entombed. [2](#source-boss-history)

Consistency does not mean every record has one preset stamped onto it. Production
changes, guitars change, and arrangements create different spectral demands.
Dismember’s melodic lines often need to remain readable above dense rhythm tracks.
The HM-2’s high-mid emphasis can help a lead cut, while its low band can compete with
bass and kick drum if left unmanaged. Recording the pedal therefore creates mixing
work as well as identity.

Entombed eventually widened its sound toward what was called death ’n’ roll.
Dismember maintained a different balance of melody, speed, and chainsaw density.
Other Swedish groups used the pedal selectively or not at all. “The Swedish sound”
was never one circuit shared without variation. It is better understood as a studio
practice that became available to a cluster of musicians.

Sunlight itself was not a fixed preset. Skogsberg recorded bands with different
budgets and ambitions, while the room and equipment changed. Repeated collaboration
made fast communication possible: a musician could ask for a known density, the
producer could establish a starting chain, and the session could spend scarce time on
performance and arrangement. That efficiency helped a local method become consistent
without making every album identical.

The academic mapping of recorded heaviness identifies the HM-2 and Sunlight layer as
one strand among several 1980s and 1990s developments. That wider view prevents
Stockholm’s method from becoming the sole origin of death-metal guitar. Florida,
Britain, Brazil, and other scenes were building different forms of density at the same
time. [6](#source-heaviness-study)

## A cheap used pedal becomes expensive evidence

After BOSS ended production in 1991, used HM-2 units remained ordinary objects for a
time. Their later association with collectible early death-metal records changed
demand. A pedal designed for broad metal use became valuable because one unintended
setting had acquired historical specificity. Japanese-made examples attracted
particular attention, though the musical importance of a unit still depends on
condition and use rather than country label alone.

Boutique builders and larger companies responded with clones, modified circuits, and
pedals that isolate or extend the HM-2 equalizer. Some add clean blends, extra mid
controls, quieter operation, or switchable clipping. These changes address practical
problems musicians met when fitting the characteristic curve into modern rigs. They
also demonstrate that “accurate” can mean two conflicting things: reproduce the
original circuit, or reproduce the remembered record after studio layering.

Reverb’s technical history traces the pedal from its initial stack-emulation goal
through Sunlight’s use and the later clone market. The story is not a failure of
design corrected by users. The original range made the extreme possible. Musicians
selected one boundary condition and gave it a context BOSS had not advertised.
[5](#source-circuit-history)

Clones expose which parts listeners think define the family. Some reproduce component
values closely. Others retain the two-band curve while changing gain, noise, or clean
blend. A parallel blend can keep pick attack beside the saturated signal; a mid
control can create room for vocals; internal voltage changes can add headroom. Each
feature weakens the claim that there is one sacred HM-2 sound while confirming that
the original equalization remains a useful reference.

## BOSS reissues its unintended legacy

In 2021 BOSS released the HM-2W under its Waza Craft line. Standard mode reproduces
the original voice; Custom mode increases drive and adjusts low and mid response.
The company retained the orange-and-black appearance and explicitly marketed the
all-controls-maximum association with Swedish death metal. A once-unofficial use had
become the product’s central documented history. [3](#source-boss-release)

The reissue changes the practical status of the sound. Players no longer need a
thirty-year-old pedal with aging components, a nonstandard power expectation, or
collector pricing. They can buy a supported production device. At the same time,
countless clones mean no company owns the general idea of boosting those frequency
regions into heavy clipping. The HM-2 name remains specific; the chainsaw vocabulary
has become a category.

The Waza version also shows what preservation means for electronics. Exact vintage
components may be unavailable or inconsistent, so engineers preserve measured
behavior with current parts and production methods. Standard mode promises continuity
at the connections a player can hear and operate; Custom mode openly departs from it.
The reissue is a working interpretation, not an untouched artifact.
Its value is practical only when musicians can still put it on a floor, step on it,
and change the surrounding chain.

That availability can flatten history into a recipe: tune down, turn four knobs right,
and receive Stockholm. The records refuse the shortcut. Cuzner brought the pedal;
other musicians recognized its possibilities; Skogsberg layered and filtered it;
bands wrote parts that made its compression useful. The box supplied an extreme
transfer curve. A scene turned that curve into musical grammar.

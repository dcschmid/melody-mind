The crucial room in early Belo Horizonte metal was not a studio. It was a record shop.
Cogumelo sold Brazilian rock and MPB alongside imported heavy-metal records that were
difficult to find elsewhere in the city. As demand for the imports grew, the shop became
a meeting point. Customers did more than buy finished music. They exchanged names,
addresses, tapes, magazines, and news about bands that might otherwise remain separated
by distance and cost. A retail counter became the fixed address of an emerging scene.
[4](#source-ufmg-roots)

That distinction matters. Sepultura's later international career can make its beginning
look inevitable, as if a successful band simply waited to be discovered. In 1984 there
was no established route from a teenage extreme-metal group in Belo Horizonte to a large
foreign label. Instruments and imported records were expensive. Specialist coverage was
scarce. The city's bands had to build a local audience while learning a fast-changing
style from partial information. Cogumelo supplied a place where those efforts could meet.

## A city learning the music sideways

Belo Horizonte was designed as a state capital, laid out on a grid and surrounded by
hills. By the early 1980s its young metal listeners were receiving sounds made elsewhere
through records, copied cassettes, radio programs, magazines, and correspondence. The
flow was uneven. A band could know the image or reputation of Venom, Motörhead, or a new
thrash group before having easy access to an entire catalog. That delay encouraged
imitation, misunderstanding, and invention at the same time.

The local field was larger than Sepultura. Academic accounts name Chakal, Holocausto,
Sarcófago, Mutilator, Overdose, Sagrado Inferno, Witch Hammer, Sextrash, The Mist, and
other groups active around the scene. They shared listeners and practical resources but
did not converge on one sound. Overdose drew from heavy metal and progressive rock.
Sarcófago pushed toward a rawer form that would circulate internationally in the extreme
underground. Chakal connected performance, radio, and Cogumelo through vocalist Vladimir
Korg. [3](#source-minas-scene) [5](#source-ufmg-chakal)

Calling all of this “Brazilian thrash” hides the instability that made the records
interesting. Thrash, death metal, black metal, punk, and older heavy metal had not settled
into clean local categories. Musicians were working out picking patterns, drum speeds,
vocal textures, English pronunciation, stage identities, and recording practice at once.
The harshness did not come from a national essence. It came from specific bands making
choices with the equipment and information available to them.

The political setting mattered without dictating one style. Brazil's military rule ended
in 1985 after a long process of abertura and public pressure. Belo Horizonte's metal
scene grew during that transition, when censorship, economic instability, and a limited
market for underground music shaped everyday possibilities. The bands did not all write
direct political programs, and their violent imagery cannot be reduced to one response
to dictatorship. The material connection is clearer: a generation was organizing
cultural space while the country's public institutions and freedoms were being
renegotiated. [3](#source-minas-scene)

## Two bands, one piece of vinyl

João Eduardo and Creuza “Pat” de Faria, the owners of Cogumelo, saw that the gathering
around the shop could support a record. Instead of financing a full album by an untested
group, the new label divided one release between two bands. Overdose recorded _Século
XX_; Sepultura recorded _Bestial Devastation_. Each received one side of the 1985 split
LP. The format spread cost and attention while giving the label two audiences to work
with. [4](#source-ufmg-roots) [2](#source-cogumelo)

The pairing also preserved contrast. Overdose's side had a different musical history and
a more elaborate sense of arrangement. Sepultura sounded younger and more abrasive.
Max Cavalera sang and played rhythm guitar, Jairo Guedz handled lead guitar, Paulo Jr.
played bass, and Iggor Cavalera played drums. The recording did not document a polished
version of a settled band. It captured a lineup still deciding what its extremity would
mean.

A split record is an argument about context. Put two bands on one object and listeners
must hear each beside the other. The label gains a catalog entry; the groups share
manufacturing and distribution; the shop has a local artifact to sell. This was less
romantic than the story of a band being found by a visionary executive. It was also more
repeatable. Cogumelo could use the response to the split to judge whether another pressing
was possible.

Iggor Cavalera later recalled that Cogumelo owners João and Pat attended Sepultura's
first show in December 1984, a performance most of the audience abandoned, and approached
the band about the Overdose split. The memory removes any illusion of a polished audition.
The label heard possibility inside a set that even the band's friends described as
terrible. [6](#source-iggor-first-show)

Sepultura followed with _Morbid Visions_ in 1986. Cogumelo also issued the _Warfare
Noise_ compilation, placing Chakal, Holocausto, Mutilator, and Sarcófago on one release.
The compilation worked like a scene map. A listener outside Minas Gerais could receive
several points of entry in one package, while the bands became mutually legible as part
of a city rather than isolated demo names. [5](#source-ufmg-chakal)

The visual language did similar work before a listener heard a note. Hand-drawn logos,
high-contrast photographs, apocalyptic paintings, and English titles located the records
inside an international metal conversation. Chakal's _Abominable Anno Domini_, for
example, used Pieter Bruegel's _The Triumph of Death_ as cover imagery. The choice joined
a sixteenth-century European painting to a young Brazilian band's record and gave local
listeners another object to decode. Research on the scene traces how such images
circulated with the music rather than treating packaging as decoration.
[5](#source-ufmg-chakal)

## The shop as an information desk

Independent distribution required conversation. A customer might ask which new import
had arrived, who was rehearsing, or how to contact a band. Musicians could learn what
listeners were buying without a formal market report. Flyers and records placed local
activity beside international releases. The shop translated a wide network into daily,
face-to-face traffic.

This did not make the scene equal. Money still determined who could buy equipment, press
a tape, or travel. The bands were overwhelmingly male, and later retellings often reduce
the work of women around shops, management, correspondence, and production to supporting
roles. Pat de Faria's part in Cogumelo is one reminder that the label's infrastructure was
not created only by the men visible on record sleeves. A useful scene history has to look
at who maintained the routes as well as who occupied the stage.

Radio added another route. Vladimir Korg's association with the _Metal Massacre_ program
gave local listeners a voice and a schedule to gather around. A song heard on air could
lead back to the shop, a show, or a tape exchange. Each medium did a different job: radio
created reach, cassettes made copying cheap, vinyl gave the music a durable commercial
object, and live shows tested whether the bands could hold a room. [5](#source-ufmg-chakal)

English lyrics were another distribution tool, though early pronunciation and grammar
could be unstable. The language connected local bands to the records they admired and
made song titles legible to tape traders abroad. It could also conceal the specific place
where the work was made. Later Sepultura records became more explicit about Brazilian
politics and history, but the early releases already carried their location through the
conditions of performance, recording, and exchange.

## From rawness to control

Sepultura's early records are sometimes praised because they sound uncontrolled. That
description misses the work inside them. Iggor Cavalera's drumming had to coordinate
speed with abrupt stops and riff changes. Max Cavalera's rhythm guitar had to make dense
distortion intelligible enough for the band to move together. Jairo Guedz supplied lead
lines within a limited recording frame. The results were rough, but roughness and
carelessness are not the same thing.

Andreas Kisser joined before _Schizophrenia_, released by Cogumelo in 1987. His arrival
expanded the guitar vocabulary and helped the band make longer structures read clearly.
The album remained aggressive while showing greater control of tempo, transitions, and
harmonized parts. That development made Sepultura easier to understand outside the
immediate scene without requiring it to become less heavy. The band's official history
places _Schizophrenia_ at the point where its reach began expanding decisively.
[1](#source-sepultura-bio) Kisser's own account describes meeting the band through a
São Paulo show, visiting Belo Horizonte, and moving there after Jairo Guedz left.
[7](#source-kisser-joining)

Foreign labels did not encounter an empty space. By the time Roadrunner became involved,
Cogumelo had already financed releases, built a catalog, and connected the band to an
audience. _Beneath the Remains_ in 1989 gave Sepultura a stronger international recording
and distribution position, but its circulation depended on years of local proof. The
global deal amplified a working structure; it did not create the band or its scene from
nothing. [8](#source-beneath-history)

## A scene does not leave town as one piece

Sepultura's ascent changed how Belo Horizonte was viewed, but the other bands did not
simply follow the same path. Sarcófago became influential through a more subterranean
international network. Chakal, Mutilator, Holocausto, and Overdose developed different
catalogs and audiences. Some lineups ended. Others continued under shifting conditions.
Cogumelo kept releasing Brazilian metal, punk, and related music rather than becoming a
single-band imprint. Its forty-year catalog records the scale of that continuity.
[2](#source-cogumelo)

There is a temptation to treat local limitation as a special ingredient that disappears
once professional resources arrive. Sepultura's later work argues against that simple
division. The band increased studio control, toured widely, and altered its rhythmic and
political language. Max and Iggor Cavalera's playing retained the physical directness of
the early years while gaining access to different production tools. Andreas Kisser and
Paulo Jr. helped carry the band through changes that would have ended a less durable
organization. [1](#source-sepultura-bio)

The scene's importance therefore cannot be measured only by how many bands became large.
It created local points of action. A listener could find a record. A musician could hear
a demo, meet another player, or learn where a show was happening. A shop could risk a
split pressing. A compilation could make several bands visible together. Mail could take
the object beyond the city.

## What the counter made possible

The shop did not literally become every part of the system. Studios captured the music,
plants pressed the vinyl, promoters arranged shows, radio carried songs, and listeners
copied tapes. Cogumelo mattered because it joined those activities at a stable address.
The counter was where imported influence met local response and where local response
could become a product with a catalog number.

That is a less dramatic origin story than sudden discovery. It is also closer to the way
independent culture often moves. The first route out of Belo Horizonte was assembled
from ordinary transactions: asking for a record, leaving a tape, recognizing repeated
demand, paying for studio time, dividing an LP, and sending copies farther than the bands
could travel. By the time Sepultura reached an international audience, the city's scene
had already done the difficult first work. It had made itself recordable.

Cogumelo's continuing catalog makes that work visible beyond Sepultura's departure for a
larger label. A scene is easy to narrate as a launchpad once one group succeeds, but a
launchpad is discarded after use. The store and label remained a point of return for
other bands, reissues, listeners, and records that never entered a global canon. The
first split LP tested whether Belo Horizonte's noise could support a repeatable local
record business, and the answer produced shelves rather than a single souvenir.

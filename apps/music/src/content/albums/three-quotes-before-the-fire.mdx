## About the Album

Three Quotes Before the Fire takes one of corporate life’s least glamorous
subjects—procurement—and turns it into a tightly written speed-metal comedy about rules,
responsibility and the moment when procedure becomes more dangerous than the problem it
was designed to control. Set between The Budget Has Been Frozen and Cloud Migration to
Nowhere, the album continues Melody Mind Music’s office-apocalypse saga with a story that
is funny because it remains painfully believable.

The crisis begins with a failing cooling system in the server room. Mara knows exactly
what is needed: one replacement part, available nearby, before the rising temperature
begins shutting down critical systems. Oliver Grant, the procurement manager, knows
something else: no purchase can be made without three comparable quotations, an approved
supplier, the correct forms and the proper signatures. Their conflict gives the album its
central tension. Mara works in minutes and degrees; Oliver works in clauses, approval
chains and audit trails.

Musically, the record moves with the same pressure as the thermometer. “Procurement
Procedure” opens with disciplined hard-rock riffing and clipped bureaucratic confidence,
presenting Oliver not as a cartoon villain but as a man who genuinely believes that
process keeps chaos outside the door. “Three Quotes Before the Fire” then drives the
central absurdity home with a chorus built to stay in the listener’s head: the room is
heating up, the fan is failing, and the company still wants three offers before anyone can
act. The humor lands because the guitars never treat the situation as a joke. The music
gives every form, delay and rejected supplier the weight of an approaching industrial
disaster.

That contrast becomes one of the album’s strongest qualities. “Approved Vendor Only” turns
a blocked supplier database into a rigid, stop-start metal groove, while “Twelve Weeks
Delivery” leans into bluesy hard rock for a negotiation where the cheapest compliant
option arrives months after it is needed. The songs are packed with specific
details—supplier categories, scoring models, signatures, card restrictions and temperature
readings—yet the writing stays direct and singable. Instead of vague corporate satire, the
album builds its world from the mechanisms that make bad decisions look responsible on
paper.

The middle section pushes the story beyond comedy. “Emergency Request Form” is funny in
its escalating chain of impossible fields, but the joke sharpens when the form demands
proof that the disaster has already happened before it will authorize action to prevent
it. “The Temperature Is Rising” accelerates into one of the album’s most urgent tracks,
placing the listener inside the server room as services are reduced, workloads are moved
and every minute carries a measurable cost. By “Out of Office Approval,” responsibility
has dissolved into automated replies and delegates without authority.

The turning point arrives with “Lowest Price, Highest Risk.” Here Oliver finally sees what
Mara has been warning him about: the purchasing model rewards the cheapest answer even
when that answer cannot arrive in time. It is the album’s moral center and one of its
strongest musical peaks, combining speed-metal attack with a broad hard-rock chorus. The
policy has not removed risk; it has merely moved risk away from the spreadsheet.

From there, the record becomes a workplace rebellion with real consequences. “Buy It
Yourself” gives Oliver the most human moment on the album. He is not transformed into a
swaggering outlaw. He checks his balance, considers his private obligations and
understands what the decision may cost him. When he pays for the part himself, the act
feels less like triumph than responsibility stripped of institutional protection. “Race to
Building B” answers with the album’s fastest burst of thrash-inflected momentum, turning
security barriers, visitor forms and closing times into obstacles in a genuine rescue run.

“Cooling the Kingdom” provides the release the album has earned. Mara and Oliver install
the replacement unit under pressure, and the music stretches the moment before the fan
finally begins to turn. Temperatures fall, services return and the server room survives.
Yet the story refuses an easy ending. “Policy Violation” places Oliver before an internal
panel, where the company is more comfortable discussing unauthorized purchasing than
confronting the system that made the purchase necessary.

The main finale, “The Cost of Waiting,” brings the album’s themes together with evidence
rather than slogans. Oliver documents every delay, every rejected approval and every
service placed at risk. The replacement part cost hundreds; the waiting cost thousands and
nearly produced a far larger failure. It is a forceful conclusion because the argument has
been built across thirteen tracks. The listener has already experienced every lost minute
before Oliver finally assigns it a financial value.

Then comes the perfect corporate epilogue. In “Four Quotes Required Next Year,” management
announces that the lesson learned from the incident is not to simplify emergency
procurement, but to demand an additional quotation. The recurring three-hit guitar motif
becomes four. It is a bleakly funny final gesture, delivered without turning the entire
album into a novelty record.

Three Quotes Before the Fire is recommended for listeners who enjoy fast riffs, narrative
albums, workplace satire and songs that understand how institutions fail through ordinary
decisions rather than grand conspiracies. Recommended if you like: Political punk,
post-punk, protest rock, anti-authoritarian lyrics, dystopian concept albums, AI-assisted
music projects. That comparison fits not because the album copies those styles, but
because it shares their distrust of systems that value obedience above human judgment.

What makes the album worth hearing is its balance. It is funny without becoming
disposable, critical without becoming preachy and technically detailed without losing
momentum. Mara and Oliver grow through conflict, the music follows the rising heat, and
each track advances the story toward a conclusion that is both satisfying and infuriating.
Three Quotes Before the Fire turns procurement policy into a metal thriller—and makes
every delayed signature feel like another degree on the thermometer.

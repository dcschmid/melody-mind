## About the Album

Le Macchine Sognano Ancora begins with an abandoned factory and a practical job. Elena, a
young restorer, is cataloguing what remains before a former Olivetti complex in Ivrea is
converted into luxury apartments and offices. Then she finds an old computer in a locked
basement. When it wakes, it does not offer software or diagnostics. It opens a memory.
Letters, employee newspapers, housing plans, punched cards, training records and fragments
of conversations begin to surface, turning the building into something far more
complicated than an industrial ruin.

That premise gives Melody Mind Music a concept album about technology that is ultimately
much more interested in people than machines. Sung entirely in Italian, Le Macchine
Sognano Ancora moves through Progressive Art Pop, retrofuturist Synth Rock, Chamber Pop
and Italian electronic music without treating those styles as decoration. Warm analog
synthesizers, Rhodes electric piano, melodic bass, dry live drums, restrained strings and
clean guitar establish a coherent musical world. A recurring four-note relay motif keeps
resurfacing in different forms until machine memory and human memory begin to feel
inseparable.

The opening stretch is deliberately tactile. “Ultimo Inventario” leads Elena through
glass, dust, labels and locked rooms before the first mysterious relay clicks beneath the
floor. “La Valentine Rossa” places a bright red typewriter at the center of the story and
turns industrial design into a question of responsibility: can an object be functional,
repairable and beautiful while respecting the person who has to use it? “Il Tesserino
Scritto a Mano” narrows the album to a single employee badge, allowing an ordinary
worker’s name, family and working day to push back against the anonymity of statistics. By
“Case di Vetro, Mattino,” the view has widened from the factory floor to the city around
it—housing, libraries, childcare and education become part of the same social
architecture.

The album’s central idea crystallizes in “La Fabbrica Appartiene Anche alla Città.”
Crucially, this is not presented as a slogan from some flawless golden age. The record
refuses to turn Olivetti’s history into a postcard. There were hierarchies, conflicts,
difficult decisions and economic pressure. What interests the album is something more
useful: the fact that industry could seriously ask what a company owes the place that
makes its success possible. What should work provide besides wages? Who gets considered
when technology changes? What happens when responsibility disappears behind the language
of efficiency?

Musically, the middle of the record becomes more restless as the story moves from
mechanical production into computation. “Schede Perforate” uses punched cards as both
physical image and rhythmic idea, while “Il Turno delle Macchine” turns retraining and
technological change into a tense conversation between old skills and new systems. Then
comes “Memoria Centrale,” the album’s major turning point, where the computer finally
answers Elena directly.

That moment could easily have become a science-fiction gimmick. Instead, the machine
remains deliberately limited. It stores, retrieves and compares; the uncomfortable
intelligence lies in the documents it has preserved. When Elena describes platform
workers, remote programmers and outsourced call-center employees, the archive does not
deliver a grand prophecy. It simply places the present beside the past and leaves the
listener to notice what changed—and what merely changed terminology.

The second half becomes sharper. “Sedie Arancioni Vuote” walks through an abandoned
cafeteria and shows industrial decline not as one spectacular collapse, but as a sequence
of reasonable-sounding removals. “Voci in Outsourcing” stretches the factory across
borders until responsibility becomes difficult to locate. “Il Curriculum Non Dorme” may be
the album’s strongest contemporary song: a portrait of permanent reskilling, ratings,
profile maintenance, short contracts and professional visibility, where the working day no
longer truly ends because employability itself has become unpaid work.

The lyrics remain grounded in screens, schedules, badges, renderings, tickets, courses and
physical spaces. That concreteness matters. Le Macchine Sognano Ancora does not need to
shout its politics because its arguments emerge from what its characters actually
experience.

“Non Voglio un Museo” provides the decisive turn. Elena rejects the easiest solution:
preserving the old factory as a tasteful memorial to a more idealistic industrial age.
Instead, she imagines a public laboratory for repair, technical education, shared tools,
programming and experimentation. “Laboratorio Aperto” then takes that idea into a meeting
room, where ownership, funding and public access have to be argued over rather than
magically resolved. There is no perfect victory and no unanimous applause. There is
something more believable: a proposal strong enough to survive disagreement.

The title track closes the album six months later. Old cafeteria tables now hold soldering
irons and broken amplifiers. Students test sensors. New manuals sit beside old technical
drawings. The red Valentine still misses one letter. The computer remains active, not as a
sacred relic but as part of a growing archive.

“Le Macchine Sognano Ancora” never seriously claims that machines dream. Its final
question is far more interesting: when technology appears to carry a dream, who put that
dream there? Who benefits from it? Who gets excluded? And who has the power to rewrite it?

Recommended if you like: Political punk, post-punk, protest rock, anti-authoritarian
lyrics, dystopian concept albums, AI-assisted music projects. The album does not sound
like a punk record, but it shares that tradition’s instinct to interrogate ownership,
authority and the language institutions use to make political or economic choices appear
inevitable.

What makes Le Macchine Sognano Ancora worth hearing is its refusal to choose between ideas
and songs. It has an argument, but it also has recurring hooks, strong visual motifs,
changing rhythmic structures and a recognizable sonic identity. The Valentine typewriter,
orange chairs, punched cards, handwritten badge, green monitor text and glass architecture
become emotional landmarks across fourteen connected chapters.

By the end, Ivrea no longer feels like a historical setting. It feels like a test case for
the digital present.

This is an album about remembering forward: asking what was lost when work became more
abstract, distributed and measurable, while refusing to pretend that the answer is simply
to rebuild the past. Its conclusion is neither nostalgia nor technological pessimism. It
is participation. Keep the machines. Keep the knowledge. Keep the uncomfortable questions.

And reopen the doors.

## About the Album

Die Häuser, die uns nicht gehören is not a record about housing policy in the abstract. It
is a record about a yellow mailbox with twelve names, a balcony full of red geraniums, a
broken lift, damp cellar walls and the moment when a polite letter turns a familiar
address into an investment asset. Across fourteen connected songs, Melody Mind Music
follows the residents of one neglected apartment building in a nameless German city as
their home is sold to an international property fund. The promised renovation arrives in
the language of improvement, but behind phrases such as “value-enhancing measure” are
surveys, scaffolding, rent increases, buyout agreements and the slow dismantling of a
community.

Musically, the album is rooted in German post-punk, but it refuses to reduce that
tradition to grey guitars and detached vocals. The bass is often the lead instrument,
moving in tense, melodic eighth-note figures while clean, angular guitar lines cut across
dry live drums. Sparse piano, cello and viola add weight without turning the songs into
orchestral drama. Metallic knocks from radiators, railings and mailboxes become part of
the rhythm rather than decorative atmosphere. A recurring motif of three short strikes
followed by one sustained note passes from pipework to bass, piano and ensemble, binding
the record together like a signal sent through the building.

The opening track, “Der gelbe Briefkasten,” establishes the album’s method immediately.
The sale is not announced with slogans or grand speeches, but with envelopes appearing
beneath twelve nameplates. From there, each song enters a different room and a different
life. “Die Küche im dritten Stock” brings together Mara, a single mother, and Tobias, a
nurse returning from night shifts, over cabbage, cocoa and the same threatening letter.
“Rote Geranien” gives the building’s oldest resident, retired tram driver Helga Krüger,
the authority of someone who remembers every former tenant by name. Her flowers are not a
quaint symbol of nostalgia; they are proof of continuity in a city that keeps replacing
memory with branding.

The writing is strongest when it lets objects carry the politics. Chalk marks on a
doorframe become a confrontation between childhood growth and property measurement. A
faded lease from 1974 becomes evidence that tenancy is also history. An old tape recorder
recovered from the cellar turns forgotten residents into witnesses. The piano that cannot
fit around the stairwell becomes one of the album’s most painful images: Nils may be
forced to leave, but the instrument remains behind, physically trapped in the architecture
that once made his life possible. None of these details feels ornamental. Each one
advances the story and deepens the sense that a home is made from repeated use, shared
inconvenience and accumulated memory.

The album also understands that solidarity rarely arrives in heroic form. It begins with
opened doors, copied phone numbers, cups placed on a kitchen table and three knocks
travelling through heating pipes. By the mid-album climax, “Zwölf Namen gegen eine
Nummer,” the residents have created an archive of leases, photographs, recordings and
testimonies. Their opponent knows the building as a portfolio entry; they answer by
learning one another’s names. That contrast gives the record its central emotional and
political charge. Resistance here is neither romanticised nor guaranteed to succeed. It is
necessary because the alternative is to disappear without a record.

That refusal of an easy victory gives the final stretch unusual force. “Aufhebungsvertrag”
follows Syrian watchmaker Samir Haddad as he signs an agreement under pressure. The song
does not judge him, nor does it pretend that leaving is always a free decision. “Die
letzte Hausversammlung” is similarly unsentimental. Some flats are saved, others are not.
Time is bought, but certainty is lost. The meeting ends not with a triumphant anthem, but
with altered contracts, departures and the knowledge that collective action has at least
forced anonymous figures to confront actual names.

The title track closes the album from the building’s perspective. New labels appear on the
mailbox. A former workshop becomes a studio. The archive moves to a library. The house
belongs to someone, but its memory does not. This final distinction is the reason the
album lingers after its last sustained note. It does not claim that memory can replace
security, affordable rent or legal protection. Instead, it argues that erasure is part of
displacement, and that documenting who lived where is itself a form of resistance.

Die Häuser, die uns nicht gehören rewards a full-album listen because its characters,
objects and musical motifs gain meaning through repetition. The red geraniums return
changed. The pipe signal moves from private contact to collective language. The yellow
mailbox shifts from an everyday object to a public record. Even the album’s quieter tracks
contribute to the pressure of the whole, allowing its louder moments to feel earned rather
than manufactured.

The alternating baritone, female alto and collective voices keep the narrative human,
distinct and grounded in the house’s shared acoustics.

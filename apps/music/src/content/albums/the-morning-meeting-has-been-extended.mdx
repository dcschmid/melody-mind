## About the Album

The Morning Meeting Has Been Extended takes one of modern working life’s least glamorous
institutions—the editorial meeting—and treats it with the seriousness of a heavy metal
crisis and the timing of a very dry office comedy. Set inside the fictional newsroom of
Beacon 24 Media, the album follows young reporter Maya Reed as her first days in
journalism collide with everything she did not imagine when she decided to become a
reporter: endless meetings, frantic homepage decisions, broken printers, dubious
performance metrics, caffeine shortages and managers who can turn almost any editorial
problem into a PowerPoint opportunity.

Musically, the album sits firmly in classic heavy metal and hard rock, with punk rock
supplying some of the impatience. Twin-guitar harmonies, muscular bass lines, physical
live-drum grooves and occasional Hammond organ give the record a deliberately traditional
backbone. That choice works because the music constantly treats ridiculous office
situations as if civilization itself were at stake. A printer jam receives the sort of
riff normally reserved for a battlefield. A KPI presentation marches forward with the
confidence of an invading army. A glowing BREAKING NEWS button becomes practically
mythological. The joke is never that heavy metal is ridiculous; the joke is how perfectly
heavy metal fits a workplace where every routine event is presented as an emergency.

The opening title track, “The Morning Meeting Has Been Extended,” establishes the tone
immediately. Maya arrives with a notebook, three pens and some remaining faith in
journalism, only to watch a discussion about municipal corruption lose the room to a
celebrity tweet. The writing is funny because it stays close to recognizable behaviour
rather than reaching for random punchlines. People debate headlines before they know what
happened. Video needs something vertical. Audience Growth wants an “engagement angle.”
Frank Mercer, Beacon 24’s exhausted editor-in-chief, quietly keeps attempting to
reintroduce the radical concept of reporting the news.

Frank becomes one of the album’s best characters. His coffee mug reads CHECK YOUR SOURCE,
a phrase that gradually develops from office decoration into the record’s moral centre.
“Check Your Source” itself could easily have become a preachy song about journalistic
integrity, but the lyrics make the point through practical details: call the office,
verify the photograph, check the registry, remove the perfect accusation if the evidence
cannot carry it. That distinction matters throughout the album. Beacon 24 is not presented
as a sinister propaganda machine staffed by villains. Most of its problems emerge from
something more believable: incentives, exhaustion, competition, dashboards and people
slowly adapting their judgement to whatever is being measured.

That becomes particularly sharp in “Clicks, Shares, Watch Time” and “Rage Engagement.” The
former turns the newsroom KPI board into one of the album’s recurring comic objects,
accumulating increasingly questionable measures while basic questions such as “Did we get
it right?” somehow remain difficult to quantify. “Rage Engagement” pushes the logic
further, following the transformation of a minor neighbourhood dispute into something
approaching civil war because angry language performs better. The humour lands, but there
is genuine bite underneath it. The album understands that clickbait does not usually
arrive with somebody announcing, “Today we abandon all standards.” It arrives one
adjective at a time.

The record’s strongest narrative turn comes with “The Story Under the Tweet.” Maya has
spent days following company registrations, payments and municipal contracts, finally
assembling a credible investigation. Then a celebrity posts something, and the
investigation disappears beneath the homepage traffic machine. The song could have stopped
at an easy complaint about audiences preferring trivia, but it does something better: the
supposedly buried story still produces tips, documents and consequences. Someone reads it.
Someone responds. Another piece of evidence appears. The point is not that serious
journalism always triumphs. It is that value and visibility are not the same measurement.

After that midpoint, the comedy becomes noticeably less comfortable. “Correction at 4:47”
is one of the album’s most effective songs because the mistake is small enough to be
plausible and large enough to matter. Beacon 24 publishes the wrong first name during a
rushed resignation story. Eight minutes later the correction is online, but the original
claim has already travelled. There is no grand conspiracy here, only three minutes of
competitive advantage and a man who suddenly has strangers calling him a crook. It gives
the album stakes without abandoning its newsroom scale.

Then comes “Coffee Is Not a Staffing Plan,” perhaps the title most likely to earn
immediate recognition from anyone who has worked in an understaffed office. The pace
slows, the jokes become more tired than frantic, and Frank finally admits that believing
in the work does not mean defending every condition under which it is performed. That song
gives the record necessary breathing room before “What Deserves the Front Page?” brings
Maya to the real question underneath almost every previous track: when multiple stories
are true, what deserves attention?

The answer, wisely, is not delivered as a heroic slogan. Editorial judgement remains
judgement. Weather warnings matter. Public money matters. Popular culture matters too. The
job is choosing, explaining and being prepared to defend the choice. That makes the final
turn considerably funnier when CEO Gordon Pike arrives to announce the grand solution to
every problem Beacon 24 has encountered: Twenty-Four Hours of Breaking News.

Gordon is not a moustache-twirling villain. He is worse for Beacon 24: he is sincerely
impressed by the dashboards. Every warning sign becomes evidence that the newsroom needs
more speed, more urgency and more “real-time content.” When Frank finally asks what Beacon
24 should do if nothing is happening, Gordon answers with the sentence that gives the
finale its title: “Then find something.”

“Then Find Something” closes the album without pretending the larger problem has been
solved. Maya is wiser, Frank is still tired, the red button is still glowing, and
management has already scheduled another meeting. But Maya’s notebook has changed. She is
asking better questions now—not only about the stories she covers, but about the system
deciding which stories receive attention.

For listeners who enjoy rock albums with characters, recurring jokes, social observation
and an actual narrative arc, this is much more than fourteen songs about office
frustration. The dystopia here simply happens to have a coffee machine, a KPI dashboard
and somebody asking whether the headline can be “more urgent.”

The Morning Meeting Has Been Extended is funny because Beacon 24 feels exaggerated by only
a few degrees. Underneath the jokes is a surprisingly affectionate argument for people who
still care about doing the work properly—even while the printer jams, the metrics climb
and management schedules another meeting to discuss why everyone has too many meetings.

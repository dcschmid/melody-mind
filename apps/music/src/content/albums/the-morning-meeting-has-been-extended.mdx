## About the Album

The Morning Meeting Has Been Extended is a hard-rock concept album about one of the least
dramatic ways a workplace can change: somebody introduces a sensible idea, it works, and
suddenly it becomes part of the furniture.

Set inside the newsroom of Current, the first album in The Newsroom Is Fine begins before
anything has gone seriously wrong. Reporters argue about homepage priorities. Editors
trust their instincts. Coffee goes cold while meetings run over. Conference Room B is
permanently occupied, somebody has a mug that reads THIS COULD HAVE BEEN AN EMAIL, and
another meeting is often created to reduce meetings. Into that familiar mess comes
Audience First, a modernization program designed to help the newsroom understand what
readers actually use.

Leo Brandt, the man bringing the audience dashboard into the room, is not a villain. His
tools solve real problems. Sara Malik finally gets evidence for digital formats older
habits had dismissed too quickly. A better headline test helps a good story reach more
people without changing the reporting. A local service story that senior editors nearly
bury turns out to be exactly what readers need. Adrian Vale, trying to keep Current
relevant without hollowing it out, has every reason to think the experiment is working.

That makes the tension across these fourteen songs believable. Nobody decides that numbers
suddenly matter more than journalism. Instead, one useful question gets added to the
morning meeting. Then another. A green arrow starts carrying more weight. Blink, a faster
competitor, appears more often in performance discussions. The dashboard moves from
experiment to newsroom display, then to the whiteboard, and finally into the daily
editorial ritual.

Musically, the album matches that shift with hard rock that feels built by people in a
room rather than assembled around a grid. The guitars are crunchy and direct, the bass is
allowed to move, and the drums push without sounding clinically corrected. The production
values late-1970s and early-1980s hard-rock economy: riffs that get to the point, clear
choruses, short melodic guitar answers, and arrangements that leave space for the lyrics.

The opening track, “Just Five Minutes,” sets the tone. Adrian arrives with a Westmark
briefing, promises the announcement will be brief, and creates the record’s first running
joke. “Conference Room B” pushes that workplace absurdity further, turning a double-booked
room into a portrait of a newsroom that could use modernization. By “Put It on the Big
Screen,” Leo has connected the dashboard and the record’s recurring three-note guitar
figure begins to attach itself to the new system—not as a warning, but as a small musical
signal that something has entered the room.

The middle stretch is where the album earns its concept. “Sara Has the Numbers” gives the
technology a human consequence: data finally gives Sara enough institutional leverage to
get a strong digital idea approved. “Green Arrow Morning” captures the satisfaction of
discovering that yesterday’s experiment worked. “Run the Other Headline” finds tension in
A/B testing while keeping a firm line between clearer presentation and clickbait. Then
“Blink Is Already There” raises the pulse, asking how Current can move faster without
confusing speed with recklessness.

Track eight, “The Story We Nearly Buried,” is the key. A practical local story about a
water-main break and bus reroutes looks minor until audience behavior makes its usefulness
impossible to ignore. The song does not argue that metrics are wiser than editors. It
makes the subtler point that editors can have blind spots, too. Adrian responds by giving
audience information a formal place in the morning process, and from that moment the album
stops being about an experiment and becomes a story about routine.

The second half gets funnier as the consequences shrink. “Put It on the Board.” “Before We
Move On.” Ben quietly adding another transformation program to his private count. The
language of change is not shouted from a stage; it appears in marker ink, calendar creep,
reopened laptops and coffee nobody gets around to drinking.

That is why “Keep the Judgment” matters. Sara and Adrian face the question the album has
been circling: what happens when a story matters editorially but the numbers say readers
probably will not care? Their answer is provisional. Use the data to improve treatment,
timing and presentation. Do not let it decide whether the journalism deserves to exist. It
is a sensible compromise, and the album refuses to guarantee it will survive.

By the title track, the new system works well enough to stay. The morning meeting is more
informed, slightly more efficient in places, noticeably longer overall—and officially
extended. The epilogue, “Green Before Coffee,” is quieter. The dashboard is already on
when people arrive. Ben puts down his mug. Sara glances at a figure. Leo fixes a cable.
Adrian starts the 8:30 meeting. Nobody comments on the fact that the screen belongs there
now.

That final image is why the album sticks. The Morning Meeting Has Been Extended is not
about a newsroom being conquered by a machine. It is about how culture changes when useful
tools become habits, habits become process, and process becomes normal before anybody
marks the exact moment it happened.

## About the Album

La España de los Kilómetros Vacíos begins with an inheritance: a ring of keys, a stack of
unpaid bills and a neglected house in a Castilian village where only seventeen people
remain. Clara arrives from Madrid expecting to inspect the property, then leave. Instead,
she encounters a place that has not vanished, but has been steadily pushed beyond the
reach of schools, healthcare, transport, housing and political attention. Across fourteen
songs, the album turns that erosion into an observed human drama.

Musically, the record is rooted in alternative folk rock, shaped by Iberian acoustic
traditions, chamber-pop detail and the patient dynamics of post-rock. Nylon-string guitar,
warm bass and dry live drums form the backbone, while viola, cello, felt piano and
electric guitar enter when the story requires them. The production rarely reaches for
spectacle. Its power comes from proximity: a key turning in a lock, chalk moving across a
forgotten blackboard, a bus leaving before sunrise, sheep bells crossing an empty road, a
kitchen lamp burning long after the square has gone dark.

The opening track, “La llave de cinta azul,” immediately establishes the album’s central
tension. Clara believes she has inherited a building; what she has really inherited is a
question. The blue-ribboned key becomes a recurring symbol of access, responsibility and
unfinished business, returning throughout the record as the story widens beyond her grief.
“Diecisiete nombres” refuses to treat the remaining residents as statistics. Each person
has a name, a routine and a reason for staying, and the song makes a demand: count
properly before declaring a place empty.

From there, the album works like an audio archive. “La escuela conserva la fecha” enters a
classroom where the final date is still written on the board. “Dos horas hasta el recreo”
follows a schoolboy whose education begins and ends with hours on a bus. “El último
rebaño” gives the final shepherd the space to speak without turning him into rural
folklore. These are not nostalgic postcards. They are songs about work, distance, policy
and the practical conditions that determine whether a life can continue in a place.

That documentary clarity is one of the album’s strengths. “Alcalde, cartero y sepulturero”
finds bleak humor in a mayor who also delivers letters and buries the dead because public
services have withdrawn faster than responsibilities have disappeared. “La cocina que
sigue encendida” shifts the focus to Mirela, a Romanian care worker sustaining an elderly
woman’s life while separated from her own family. The song dismantles the fantasy that
rural survival rests only on tradition. It also depends on migrant labor, care work,
contracts, housing and time off.

The turning point arrives with “Aquí todavía queda alguien.” Clara plays her recordings
inside the closed community hall, and the village hears itself as a shared archive for the
first time. The chorus is not a triumphant slogan. It is a statement of continued
existence: someone still lights the stove, waits for the bus, counts the flock and answers
the door. The arrangement grows from close acoustic detail into a broad communal
performance, but the song resists easy uplift. Being heard is not the same as being saved.

The darker second half makes that distinction clear. “Casas vacías, puertas cerradas”
exposes the contradiction of villages filled with unusable homes: disputed inheritances,
absent owners, damaged roofs and rents that newcomers cannot risk. “Noventa minutos” turns
distance into danger when Teresa needs urgent medical care. The road to the hospital is
not presented as bad luck or geography alone, but as the result of decisions made
elsewhere. Then “El billete de las 18:40” gives Clara her most honest moment. Staying
might be commitment, but it might also be guilt, vanity or the desire to cast herself as a
rescuer.

The album earns its final act by rejecting that rescuer narrative. “La lista sobre la
mesa” replaces inspiration with a budget, a work schedule and assigned responsibilities.
The former municipal building will not become a miracle. It will become something more
credible: a shared room with electricity, desks, books, recording equipment, internet
access and a door that opens. “La casa del concejo” captures the work of making that
possible, from broken wiring and damp walls to donated shelves and delayed broadband
installation.

“Veintiuna luces” closes the album with four new residents and no illusion that the
village has been restored. The doctor is still far away. The school journey is still too
long. People may leave again. Yet twenty-one illuminated windows now answer the darkness
where there were seventeen before. The result is hopeful because it remains measurable,
provisional and vulnerable.

Recommended if you like: Political punk, post-punk, protest rock, anti-authoritarian
lyrics, dystopian concept albums, AI-assisted music projects. This is not a punk record in
sound, but it shares protest music’s instinct for naming systems rather than blaming
individuals. Its anger is carried by detail, its politics by lived consequences, and its
optimism by collective work rather than grand declarations.

La España de los Kilómetros Vacíos is worth hearing from beginning to end because every
song changes the meaning of the one before it. It is a record about depopulation, but also
about who is allowed to remain, what a functioning community requires and why a place
cannot survive on scenery alone. Thoughtful, melodic and forceful, it gives rural Spain
neither a funeral nor a fantasy. It gives it voices, contradictions and one open door.
